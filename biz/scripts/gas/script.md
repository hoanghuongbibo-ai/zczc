## SETUP: THE MOST IMPORTANT WATERWAY ON EARTH

[VISUAL: Map zooming into the Strait of Hormuz]

To understand your gas bill, you have to understand one narrow stretch of water.

The Strait of Hormuz sits between Iran and Oman. It's the only sea route out of the Persian Gulf. And in normal times, about a fifth of the world's oil passes through it. [S8][S9]

Saudi oil. Iraqi oil. Kuwaiti oil. Emirati oil. Qatari gas. All of it squeezes through this one channel.

Now, you might be thinking: wait, doesn't America produce its own oil?

We do. In fact, we produce more than anyone. In 2025, the U.S. pumped about 13.6 million barrels a day, a record, roughly 40% more than Russia or Saudi Arabia. [S10]

[VISUAL: Bar chart: U.S. vs Russia vs Saudi crude output, 2025]

But here's the catch nobody puts on a campaign sign. Oil is sold on a global market at a global price. When a fifth of the world's supply gets stuck behind a war, a barrel pumped in Texas goes up in price too, because a buyer in Tokyo or Berlin will pay more for it.

Producing your own oil isn't the same as controlling its price.

Keep that in mind, because it explains almost everything that happened next.

## CHAPTER 2: THE SHOCK

[VISUAL: Animated tanker traffic map going dark]

Iran struck back, and it went after the one thing that could hurt the whole world economy at once: shipping.

Shortly after the war began, Iran took effective control of the strait, and shipping through it largely stopped. [S8]

Then the insurers made it worse. Before the war, war-risk insurance to cross a normal stretch of ocean cost about one-thousandth of one percent of a ship's value. By June, crossing Hormuz cost about 4% of the ship's value for a single seven-day policy. [S14]

[VISUAL: Insurance premium graphic: 0.001% → 4%]

That's roughly 4,000 times more, just for the insurance to sail.

Oil exploded. Before the war, a barrel traded around $70. Within about ten days, it spiked to nearly $120. [S9]

ABC News later described it as the largest oil supply shock on record. [S7]

Governments panicked, and they reached for the emergency stash. On March 11th, the International Energy Agency's members agreed to release 400 million barrels from their strategic reserves. [S15] To put that in perspective, the previous record, set after Russia invaded Ukraine, was 182 million barrels. [S15] This was more than double the biggest release in history.

It wasn't enough. The IEA's own chief, Fatih Birol, said the real key was "the resumption of transit through the Strait of Hormuz." [S9] You can't release your way out of a blocked highway.

## CHAPTER 3: "IF THEY RISE, THEY RISE"

[VISUAL: Pump price ticker climbing]

Now the shock hit your wallet.

On March 5th, five days into the war, gas averaged $3.25. [S3] That's when Reuters asked the President about it. Here's his full answer, in context, because context matters.

He said he didn't have "any concern about it." He said prices would "drop very rapidly when this is over." And then: "If they rise, they rise." He called the military operation "far more important than having gasoline prices go up a little bit." [S3]

That's a defensible position if you believe the war was necessary. Presidents make tradeoffs. But "a little bit" didn't hold up.

By March 26th, the national average was $3.98, up a full dollar in one month. [S16]

By early May, it was $4.45, up 35 cents in a single week. [S17]

And on May 21st, ahead of Memorial Day, it hit $4.56. Every state in the country was above four dollars. [S4]

[VISUAL: U.S. map turning red, state by state]

That fed straight into inflation. In April, consumer prices were up 3.8% from a year earlier, the highest since 2023. [S18] In May, it was 4.2%, and gasoline alone was up more than 40% from a year earlier. [S19]

## CHAPTER 4: WHY TEXAS DIDN'T SAVE US

[VISUAL: Permian Basin drilling rigs, B-roll]

So this is where you'd expect America's record oil industry to ride to the rescue. Higher prices, more drilling, more supply, lower prices. That's how it's supposed to work.

It didn't, at least not fast. And the reasons tell you a lot about how the industry works today.

First, shale companies have spent years promising Wall Street discipline: steady output, and cash back to shareholders instead of drilling sprees. Energy publication Argus reported that the windfall from higher prices was more likely to go to dividends and buybacks than new wells. ConocoPhillips' CEO said the company didn't want to "whipsaw" its plans up or down. [S20]

Second, drillers didn't trust the price. Today's oil was expensive, but oil for delivery a year or two out was much cheaper. Why spend billions on new wells if the war might end next month?

And third, even when companies do decide to drill, analysts at J.P. Morgan said new supply takes several months to arrive. [S20]

Let's be fair to the industry. Those are rational business decisions. Companies that drilled like crazy during past price spikes often went bankrupt when prices crashed. But it means the safety net Americans think they have, "we make our own oil," doesn't work the way we assume when it matters most.

## CHAPTER 5: THE DEAL, AND THE DROP THAT DIDN'T COME

[VISUAL: Trump's Truth Social post on screen]

Then, in mid-June, the turning point. The U.S. and Iran announced a deal to end the war and reopen the strait. [S8]

The President celebrated on social media: "Ships of the World, start your engines. Let the oil flow!" [S8]

And oil did fall. By late June, international crude had dropped back to roughly where it was before the war. [S21]

So did your gas fall back to $2.98?

No. On June 24th, gas still averaged $3.91. [S21]

[VISUAL: Two lines on a chart: oil dropping fast, gas barely moving]

And here's where the story gets interesting, because the person who got angriest about it was the President himself.

On Truth Social, Trump wrote: "The big Oil Companies are not dropping their price at the pump commensurate with the sharply lower prices they are paying for Oil. In other words, customers are being 'gouged.'" He said he'd told the Justice Department to investigate. [S21]

The industry pushed back. The American Petroleum Institute said: "Gasoline prices don't move in lockstep with crude oil." Chevron's CFO said companies were "doing everything we can," but that "it's going to take time." [S21] Since then, the Justice Department and the Federal Trade Commission have urged state attorneys general to look into gas pricing, but we haven't found any charges or findings so far. [S34]

So who's right?

Economists have a name for this. They call it "rockets and feathers." Prices go up like a rocket and float down like a feather.

[VISUAL: Animated rocket and feather]

The classic research on this comes from economists Severin Borenstein, Colin Cameron and Richard Gilbert, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 1997. They found that when crude oil goes up, nearly all of the increase reaches the pump within about four weeks. When crude goes down, the drop takes about eight weeks to pass through. [S22]

But here's the detail most people miss. In their data, wholesale gasoline prices tracked oil pretty evenly. The biggest lag was at the last step, between wholesale and the price on the sign at your gas station. They suggested this likely reflected short-run market power among retail sellers. [S22]

Now, that research is from decades ago, and we haven't seen a study that measures this exact war. But it means "big oil is gouging you" and "it just takes time" can both be partly true. The delay is real, and the cost of that delay lands on you.

## CHAPTER 6: THE SECOND SHOCK

[VISUAL: News footage of a damaged tanker]

Then the peace fell apart.

In late June and early July, ships in and around the strait were hit by drones and projectiles, including a Qatari gas tanker and a Saudi supertanker. On July 8th, President Trump said the truce was "over." The U.S. resumed strikes. [S23]

By mid-August, the 60-day ceasefire had expired with no deal. [S24] Oil climbed back above $90, [S24] then around $100. [S25]

And in September, things got worse. AAA said Labor Day travelers faced "the highest gas prices ever for this time of year." [S25] On September 22nd, diesel hit $6.53 a gallon, an all-time national record, beating the peak set after Russia invaded Ukraine. [S5][S26] Diesel moves trucks, trains, and farm equipment, so that cost shows up on almost everything you buy. [S26]

[VISUAL: Diesel price chart, record line marked]

The month of September averaged $4.33 for regular gas, the most expensive September on record, about 50 cents above the old record. [S5]

And the war kept spreading. Houthi forces attacked Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, one of the main routes to get oil around the strait, and it temporarily shut down. [S7]

The economy felt it. On September 16th, the Federal Reserve raised interest rates for the first time since 2023, to between 3.75% and 4%. [S27] So the war showed up twice: once at the pump, and again on your credit card and mortgage rates.

## CHAPTER 7: WHO WON, WHO PAID

[VISUAL: Split screen: oil company logos vs. a family's receipt]

So let's add it up.

**Who paid.** Researchers at Brown University built a tracker comparing actual fuel prices against what they estimate prices would have been with no war. By Labor Day, they found Americans had spent more than $100 billion extra on gasoline and diesel, or over $750 per household. [S28] Moody's chief economist Mark Zandi, using a slightly different method that also includes jet fuel, put it at about $115 billion, roughly $860 per household. [S29]

[VISUAL: "$750–$860 per household" stat card]

That's an estimate, not a receipt. But either way, it's like a surprise bill that showed up for every family in America.

**Who earned.** On July 31st, the two biggest American oil companies reported their spring results. Chevron earned $12.1 billion in the second quarter, up from $2.5 billion a year earlier. [S30] ExxonMobil earned $14.5 billion, more than triple what it made in the first quarter. [S31] Spread across the quarter, that's roughly $160 million a day for Exxon.

And where did a lot of that money go? Back to shareholders. Exxon returned $9.4 billion in the quarter, including $5.1 billion in stock buybacks. Chevron returned about $6.6 billion. [S30][S31]

To be fair: oil companies don't set the global price of oil, and they lose money when prices crash. Their profits are a result of the war, not proof that they caused it. But it does show who sits on which side of a price shock.

**Who decides.** Here's something that surprised me. Since the war began, Congress has voted 20 times on war powers resolutions. [S32] The House has passed them. On September 24th, the Senate came within a single vote: it failed 50 to 49. [S32]

Supporters say Congress, not the President, should decide whether America goes to war, especially a war that has lasted more than seven months. Opponents, like Senate Foreign Relations Chairman Jim Risch, have argued it's a limited operation. Early on he said, "This is not a forever war, indeed not even close to it." [S13]

Wherever you stand on that, the war is now part of your monthly budget.
