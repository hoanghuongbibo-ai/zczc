# Why Americans Can't Buy a House Until 40 — Full Script

**Target:** ~20 minutes (about 3,600 words), matching the length and first-person voice of the channel's past videos.
**Disclaimer (on screen + spoken at end):** Educational only, not individualized financial advice.

---

## COLD OPEN

So last November, a number went viral. You've probably seen it. The typical first-time homebuyer in America is now 40 years old. Forty. That's a record.

[VISUAL: Screenshots of the headlines, "The big four-oh"]

In 1991, that same buyer was 28. So in one generation, buying your first house went from something you did in your twenties to something you do after your first gray hair. And the comments were exactly what you'd expect. Millennials spend too much on avocado toast. Gen Z doesn't want to work. Boomers pulled up the ladder.

But here's what nobody in those comments noticed. That number is probably wrong.

Because when you line up the actual mortgage records, the credit bureau data, the Census numbers, almost none of them say 40. They say early-to-mid thirties.

So, case closed? Everyone's fine?

No. Because once I figured out why that number went viral, and what the real data says instead, I found something way worse than "you'll buy at 40." The real story is about who gets to buy at all. And starting about three weeks ago, the door got even narrower.

---

## SETUP: WHERE THE "40" CAME FROM

[VISUAL: NAR 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers cover]

The 40 comes from the National Association of Realtors. That's the trade group for real estate agents. Every year they publish something called the Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers, and in November 2025 it said the median first-time buyer was 40, and that first-time buyers were only 21% of the market. Both records.

NAR's deputy chief economist, Jessica Lautz, put it like this: "It means 10 years of lost housing wealth gains for first-time homebuyers."

Heavy stuff. Every news outlet in the country ran it.

But how did they get that number? NAR actually tells you, right in their own release. They mailed out surveys. They got back 6,103 of them. That's a response rate of 3.5%.

[VISUAL: "6,103 responses / 3.5% response rate" on screen]

Now think about who fills out a long paper survey that shows up in the mail. Is it the 29-year-old who just closed on a condo and is drowning in moving boxes? Or is it the 45-year-old who actually opens their mail?

That's not just me being a smartass. The American Enterprise Institute's Housing Center looked at the same question using credit bureau data from the New York Fed, which is based on a random sample of credit reports. Their answer for 2025: a median first-time buyer age of 34.

And this past July, the Mortgage Bankers Association lined up basically every data source that exists.

[VISUAL: Table — NAR 40 / mortgage records 32 / mortgage applications 32 / Census American Housing Survey 33 / credit data 34 / Redfin 35]

Mortgage records? 32. Mortgage applications? 32. The Census Bureau's own housing survey? 33. Credit data? 34. Redfin, using government survey data? 35. And then there's NAR, way out on its own, at 40.

The MBA's conclusion was that the typical first-time buyer today "is likely not much older than one a decade ago."

So, fake news, right? Everybody can calm down?

Here's the problem. If the age didn't really change that much, then something else did. Because the feeling that went viral with that number, the feeling that buying a house is getting impossible, that part shows up in every single dataset I looked at.

## CHAPTER 1: THE MATH STOPPED WORKING

Let's do a little experiment.

Let's say you're a totally normal American household. Not rich, not broke. You make exactly the typical household income, which according to Redfin was $87,599 as of this summer.

[VISUAL: Animated character "You," with a $87,599 tag]

And you want to buy a totally normal house. The median existing home in America sold for $429,100 in August.

[VISUAL: Median house, $429,100 price tag]

Now, back in the 1990s, the typical home cost about 3.2 times the typical income. That's according to Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies. By 2019, it was 4.1. By 2024, it was 5.0. And in their 2026 report, Harvard says it's still "nearly five times" the median household income.

[VISUAL: Bar chart — 1990s 3.2x / 2019 4.1x / 2024 5.0x]

Put that in human terms. If houses still cost what they cost relative to income back in the '90s, your house would be about $280,000. Instead, it's $429,100.

[NOTE FOR EDITOR: $87,599 × 3.2 ≈ $280,317. Calculation, not a sourced figure. See fact-check notes.]

And here's the stat that should make you mad. Between 2019 and 2024, Harvard found home prices went up 48%. Incomes went up 22%. Prices ran more than twice as fast as your paycheck.

So how much would you actually need to make to afford this house? Redfin ran exactly that math. Using the rule that housing shouldn't eat more than 30% of your income, with 15% down, you'd need to earn $109,796 a year.

You make $87,599. You're $22,197 short. Every single year.

[VISUAL: Gap bar, $22,197 in red]

Now, to be fair, Redfin says that gap actually got a little smaller over the past year. It was over $28,000 two years ago. Their senior economist Yingqi Xu said, quote, "The earnings needed to buy a house have stabilized after several years of deterioration, but that doesn't mean homes are affordable to the average American."

Hold on to that word, "stabilized." Because that was before September.

But first, the bigger question. How did houses get this expensive in the first place? Because it's not like people suddenly wanted houses more. And that's where this story goes back almost 20 years.

---

## CHAPTER 2: THE DECADE AMERICA STOPPED BUILDING

[VISUAL: 2008 archival footage — foreclosure signs, half-built subdivisions]

In 2008, the housing market blew up. You know this part. Risky mortgages went bad, the market collapsed, and the whole economy went down with it.

But here's the part people forget. After the crash, America basically stopped building homes.

According to the St. Louis Fed, in 2005 the country issued 7.3 building permits for every 1,000 people. By 2009, it was 1.9. That is a collapse of almost three-quarters in four years.

[VISUAL: Line chart of building permits per 1,000 people, 1972–2024]

And it never really came back. In 2024, it was 4.3 per 1,000 people. That's still 35% below the average from 1960 to 2000.

So think about what that means. For over a decade, the country kept adding people, kept forming new households, and just didn't build enough homes for them. The St. Louis Fed estimates the cumulative shortfall since 2008 at 3 to 5 million homes.

Now, I want to be careful here, because economists actually fight about this number. The Congressional Research Service lined up the big estimates. Freddie Mac says 3.7 million. Zillow says 4.7 million. Brookings says 4.9 million. The National Association of Realtors says 5.5 million.

[VISUAL: Shortage estimates side-by-side]

And then some economists, like a pair of researchers at the Cato Institute, argue these models are so sensitive to their assumptions that a small tweak can turn a shortage into a surplus. CRS itself says the shortage might be more about specific places and specific kinds of housing than the whole country.

But here's one number that's hard to argue with. In 2024, the homeowner vacancy rate hit 0.95%, according to the St. Louis Fed. That's the lowest on record. Basically, there was nothing for sale.

So why didn't builders just build more? The St. Louis Fed points to four things. Not enough construction workers. Rising costs for land, materials, and labor. Zoning rules and slow permitting. And not enough buildable land near where the jobs are.

So that's the first thing that went wrong. Not enough houses got built.

But that alone doesn't explain why things got so much worse after 2022. For that, you need the second thing. And it's one of the weirdest traps in the entire economy.

---

## CHAPTER 3: THE GOLDEN HANDCUFFS

During the pandemic, mortgage rates fell to the lowest levels ever recorded. In January 2021, Freddie Mac's average 30-year rate hit 2.65%. By early 2022, about a quarter of all mortgages in America had a rate below 3%. If you got one of those, congratulations, you won the lottery.

[VISUAL: Mortgage rate chart 2019–2022, falling to ~3%]

Then inflation hit, and mortgage rates shot up. Even today's rate, which we'll get to, is nearly triple that 2021 low.

And now think about it from the homeowner's side. Let's say your family outgrows your house. You want to sell and move up. But your current mortgage is at 3%, and a new one is at 7%. Moving doesn't just mean a bigger house. It means a way bigger rate on a way bigger loan.

So what do you do? You stay. Everybody stays.

Economists call this the lock-in effect, and the Federal Housing Finance Agency actually measured it. Their researchers found that for every percentage point that market rates sit above your current rate, the chance you sell your house drops by 18.1%.

[VISUAL: FHFA working paper title page; "−18.1% per point" graphic]

Add that up across the country, and they estimate lock-in prevented about 1.33 million home sales between mid-2022 and late 2023.

And here's the twist that nobody expected. Higher rates are supposed to make houses cheaper, right? Fewer buyers, lower prices. And the FHFA found that did happen, with higher rates on their own knocking prices down by about 3.3%. But because nobody was selling, the shrinking supply pushed prices up by about 5.7%. The lock-in more than canceled out the cool-down.

So the thing that was supposed to make houses affordable made them more expensive.

How many people are still in these handcuffs? According to FHFA's National Mortgage Database, at the end of 2025, about half of all mortgages in America, 50.6%, still had a rate below 4%. Nearly one in five were below 3%.

[VISUAL: Pie chart — 50.6% below 4%]

And you can see the result everywhere. In 2025, Americans bought just 4.06 million existing homes. That's the lowest since 1995. Thirty years ago.

And NAR's own data shows people now stay in their homes a median of 11 years before selling. Also a record.

## CHAPTER 4: WHO'S SITTING IN THE BIG HOUSES

So if nobody's selling, who's living in all the family-sized houses?

Redfin looked at Census data on homes with three or more bedrooms. Empty-nest baby boomers, so older people whose kids have moved out, own 28.2% of them. Millennials with kids? 14.2%. Gen Zers with kids? 0.3%.

[VISUAL: Big house icons split 28.2% vs 14.2%]

So boomers without kids at home own about twice as many big houses as millennials who actually have kids.

Now before the comments turn into a generational war, let's be fair here. This isn't some evil boomer plot. It's math. Redfin found that 54% of boomer homeowners have no mortgage at all. The median monthly housing cost for those owners was $612.

$612 a month. For a house.

Redfin's senior economist Sheharyar Bokhari said it pretty plainly: "Boomers don't have much motivation to sell, financially or otherwise. They typically have low housing costs."

If you were paying $612 a month, you wouldn't move either. Nobody's doing anything wrong. But the result is a housing market that's frozen in place. The people who already own are staying put, and the people who don't own can't get in.

And for a moment there, it looked like that was finally about to change.

---

## CHAPTER 5: THE THAW THAT DIDN'T HAPPEN

At the end of 2025, mortgage rates started falling. By January 2026, the average 30-year rate was around 6.1%, close to its lowest level in more than three years, according to Freddie Mac.

[VISUAL: Rate chart dipping toward 6%]

And buyers came back. In December 2025, existing home sales hit an annual pace of 4.35 million, the fastest in nearly three years. Redfin's numbers showed the affordability gap shrinking. It looked like the market was finally starting to thaw.

But there was a problem.

On September 16, 2026, the Federal Reserve did something it hadn't done since 2023. It raised interest rates. A quarter of a point, to a range of 3.75% to 4%. The vote was unanimous. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said the Fed "removed a dose of accommodation," pointing to what he called a "geopolitical landscape of shocks and uncertainty."

[VISUAL: Fed press conference footage, September 16, 2026]

And mortgage rates kept climbing. According to Freddie Mac, the average 30-year fixed rate was 7.03% the week of September 24. By October 1, it was 7.28%. A year earlier, it was 6.34%.

So let's go back to our normal household and that $429,100 house. Say you put down 10%, which is the median down payment for first-time buyers according to NAR. That's $42,910 in cash just to get in the door.

At January's rate of about 6.1%, your monthly principal and interest would be roughly $2,340. At today's 7.28%, it's roughly $2,642.

[VISUAL: Side-by-side monthly payments, $2,340 vs $2,642]

[NOTE FOR EDITOR: Both payments are calculated (30-year fixed, $386,190 loan, principal and interest only, no taxes or insurance). See fact-check notes.]

That's about $300 more every month. About $3,600 a year. For the exact same house. And that's before property taxes and insurance, which Harvard says have been squeezing homeowners too.

And the people sitting on those 3% mortgages? Now the gap between their rate and the market rate just got bigger. Which, if you remember the FHFA math, makes them even less likely to sell.

The sales numbers are already slipping. In August, existing home sales fell to an annual pace of 3.98 million. NAR's chief economist Lawrence Yun said, "Mortgage rates and home sales move in opposite directions, so it's not surprising to see a mild dip."

So the thaw is on ice. Again.

## CHAPTER 6: WASHINGTON'S FIXES

Okay, so what's the government doing about it? Two big ideas have come out of the White House in the past year. And both of them are more complicated than the headlines.

**Idea number one: blame Wall Street.**

On January 20, 2026, President Trump signed an executive order called "Stopping Wall Street from Competing with Main Street Homebuyers."

[VISUAL: Executive order title page]

Now the headlines said Trump "banned" big investors from buying homes. That's not exactly what it does. According to the law firm Cooley, the order doesn't ban anything outright or force anyone to sell. It directs federal agencies to write new guidance and rules to restrict sales of single-family homes to large institutional investors. And it exempts homes that were built to be rentals from the start.

So how big are these investors, really? A fact-check by EconoFact found that large institutional investors own about 3% to 3.8% of the single-family rental stock nationally, depending on whose estimate you use. But in the 20 metro areas where they're most active, it's 12.4%. And they're buying less than 2% of all homes, according to John Burns Research and Consulting.

So in certain cities, big investors can matter a lot. Nationally, they're a small slice. Supporters of the order say big investors shouldn't be competing with first-time families at all. Critics, including researchers at AEI writing about a similar proposal in the Senate, argue that pushing investors out could cut the supply of rental housing and end up hurting lower-income families. Both sides have a point, and this fight isn't over.

**Idea number two: make the mortgage longer.**

In November 2025, President Trump and Federal Housing Finance Agency director Bill Pulte floated the idea of a 50-year mortgage.

The pitch is simple. Stretch the loan out, and the monthly payment goes down. And that part is true. On our $429,100 house, at today's rate, a 50-year loan would drop the payment from about $2,642 to about $2,407. You save around $235 a month.

[VISUAL: 30-year vs 50-year total interest bars]

But look at the total interest. On a 30-year loan, you'd pay about $565,000 in interest over the life of the loan. On a 50-year loan, it's about $1.06 million.

[NOTE FOR EDITOR: Calculated figures. See fact-check notes.]

Realtor.com's senior economist Joel Berner put it this way: "A 50-year mortgage results in almost double the interest payments of a 30-year mortgage and a longer path to meaningful home equity."

And a lot of critics point out the bigger problem. If everyone can suddenly afford a slightly bigger monthly payment, sellers can just raise prices. You end up with the same house, a higher price, and a debt you're paying off until you're 80.

Which brings us back to the real question. If the age of first-time buyers barely changed, and most people still buy in their early thirties, then who exactly is getting through this door?

## CHAPTER 7: WHO ACTUALLY GETS IN

Here's what I think is the most important number in this whole video.

[VISUAL: Slow reveal: "24%"]

In a 2025 survey, Redfin found that 24% of Gen Z and millennial homebuyers used family money for their down payment. 21% got cash gifts from family. 11% used an inheritance.

So yeah, people are still buying in their early thirties. But a big chunk of them are getting there with help from Mom and Dad. Some headlines started calling them "nepo-homebuyers."

Now, to be fair, the most common way young buyers paid was still saving from their own paychecks. 57% did that. People are grinding. But think about what it means when about one in four needed family money to make it work.

It means the question isn't really "how old will you be when you buy a house?" It's more like "does your family already own one?"

And the people who don't have that help? They're the ones disappearing from the data. According to the Census Bureau, the homeownership rate for households under 35 fell to 35.2% in the second quarter of 2026. That's down 1.2 points from a year earlier, the steepest drop of any age group.

And this is where it stops being about houses and starts being about wealth. According to the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, in 2022 the median homeowner had a net worth of about $396,000. The median renter? $10,400.

[VISUAL: Two stacks of money, $396,000 vs $10,400]

That's roughly 38 times more. And for homeowners, the single biggest piece of that wealth is the house itself.

Meanwhile, Harvard found that nearly half of all renter households in 2024 were cost-burdened, meaning more than 30% of their income went to housing. So the people who most need to build wealth are spending the biggest share of their paycheck on rent, which makes it even harder to save a down payment, which keeps them renting.

That's the loop.

## ENDING: WHY IT MATTERS

So let's go back to that viral number. Is the typical first-time buyer in America 40 years old?

Probably not. The best data we have says it's somewhere in the early-to-mid thirties, and that's not that different from a decade ago.

But here's why I think that number went viral anyway. It felt true. Because for a lot of people, the honest answer to "when will you buy a house?" isn't 40. It's "I don't know if I ever will."

[VISUAL: Montage — the house, the rate chart, the 24%, the $10,400]

What actually changed isn't the age. It's the price of the ticket. Homes went from about three times income to about five. The country spent a decade not building enough. Half the homeowners in America are locked into rates they'll never give up. And just as things started to thaw, rates went right back up.

So the ladder didn't disappear. It just got a lot steeper, and more and more of the people climbing it are getting a boost from someone who already made it to the top.

And when the main way regular Americans build wealth starts depending on who your parents are, that's not just a housing problem. That's the American Dream quietly turning into an inheritance.

[VISUAL: Disclaimer card]

Quick note: this video is for education only. It's not financial advice, and nobody knows where rates or home prices go next. But if you want to understand why it feels like the game is rigged, now you know where to look.
