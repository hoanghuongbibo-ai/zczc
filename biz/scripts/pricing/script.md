# Your Price Isn't My Price: Full Script

**Target:** ~18 minutes (about 2,700 spoken words), first-person voice matching past videos.
**Disclaimer (spoken at end):** Educational only. Not legal or financial advice.

---

## COLD OPEN

On a Thursday in September 2025, more than 40 strangers got on the same video call.

[VISUAL: Grid of blurred video-call tiles]

They all opened the Instacart app. They all picked the same Safeway in Washington, D.C. They all picked the same carton of eggs, a dozen Lucerne. They all chose pickup. And at the same moment, they all looked at the price.

A couple of them saw $3.99. Others saw $4.28, $4.59, or $4.69. A few saw $4.79.

[VISUAL: Five egg cartons side by side, $3.99 / $4.28 / $4.59 / $4.69 / $4.79]

Same eggs. Same store. Same minute. Five different prices.

Nobody did anything different. So who decided that one person pays 80 cents more for breakfast?

That video call was part of an investigation into one of the biggest grocery apps in America. About a week after it came out, federal regulators started asking that company questions. Within two weeks, the company shut down the program behind it.

But Instacart is only where this story starts. Because once I dug into how this works, I found out the eggs were the innocent version. The technology to charge you, specifically you, a different price than me already exists. Some apps are already legally required to admit they're using your personal data to set prices. And as of October 1, in one state, a version of this became illegal.

So let's figure out who's setting your price, and how much they know about you when they do it.

---

## SETUP: SAME CART, DIFFERENT PRICE

[VISUAL: Report cover, "Same Cart, Different Price"]

The investigation came from three groups: Consumer Reports, the Groundwork Collaborative, which is a progressive economic policy group, and the progressive media outlet More Perfect Union. They published it on December 9, 2025, under the title "Same Cart, Different Price."

Here's what they did. They recruited volunteers in four cities: North Canton, Ohio; Saint Paul, Minnesota; Seattle; and Washington, D.C. They had people shop for the same 20 grocery items, at the same store, at the same time, mostly at Safeway and Target.

And here's what they found.

Nearly three-quarters of the items they tested showed different prices to different shoppers. Some items had up to five different prices at the same time. For basics like cereal, pasta, and peanut butter, the highest price was up to 23% above the lowest.

[VISUAL: Price pairs — Skippy at Target, Ohio: $2.99 / $3.59; Wheat Thins at Safeway, Seattle: $3.99 / $4.89; Cheerios at Target, St. Paul: $3.99 / $4.59]

Skippy peanut butter at a Target in Ohio? $2.99 for some people, $3.59 for others. Wheat Thins at a Safeway in Seattle? $3.99 or $4.89. Cheerios at a Target in Saint Paul? $3.99 or $4.59.

And it wasn't just the shelf price. Sometimes the sale price stayed the same, but the "original" price changed. So the discount looked bigger for some people than for others, even though they were paying the same thing.

Add it all up, and the total for the exact same cart, from the exact same store, at the exact same time, varied by about 7% on average. The researchers took that 7% and applied it to what a family of four typically spends on groceries, and estimated that could mean a swing of about $1,200 a year.

[VISUAL: "$1,200 / year" counter]

Now, before we go further, I need to be really clear about one thing, because it matters for the rest of this video.

The researchers did not find evidence that Instacart was setting prices based on your income, your ZIP code, or your shopping history. And Instacart said its tests were "never based on personal or behavioral characteristics." It said the tests were randomized, short-term, and run by the stores, which set their own prices.

So this wasn't Instacart reading your mind. It was something else. And honestly, once you understand what it was, it's still pretty wild.

## CHAPTER 1: YOU'RE IN AN EXPERIMENT

In 2022, Instacart bought a company called Eversight for $59 million.

[VISUAL: Eversight logo, "$59M, 2022"]

Eversight makes software that lets retailers test prices. Basically, show some shoppers one price and other shoppers a higher or lower price, then watch who still buys. It's the same idea as a tech company testing two versions of a button, except the button is your grocery bill.

Instacart's CEO at the time, Fidji Simo, told investors the technology helps retailers optimize prices based on how price-sensitive customers are. That's according to the Associated Press.

So the goal is to figure out the highest price people will still pay.

And here's the line that got people angry. Groundwork found it right on Instacart's own website, describing how the system works: "End shoppers are not aware that they're in an experiment."

[VISUAL: Quote on screen, highlighted]

Think about that from your side. You're ordering groceries on a Tuesday night. You see $4.79 for eggs. You figure that's just what eggs cost now, because everything's expensive. You have no way of knowing the person next to you is seeing $3.99.

You're not a customer in that moment. You're a data point.

And the thing is, this idea isn't new at all. It's actually older than the iPhone.

## CHAPTER 2: AMAZON TRIED THIS IN 2000

[VISUAL: Archival Amazon homepage, 2000]

Between August 31 and September 5, 2000, Amazon randomly changed the prices on 68 DVDs. Same movie, different customers, different prices.

The goal was exactly what Eversight does: figure out how price changes affect sales. But customers noticed they were paying different prices for the same DVD, and they were not happy.

Amazon backed down fast. It refunded about 6,900 customers an average of $3.10 each. And it put out a statement saying, "In retrospect, this random testing was a mistake, and we regret it."

Jeff Bezos added: "We've never tested and we never will test prices based on customer demographics."

[VISUAL: Bezos quote card, CNN Money, Sept. 28, 2000]

So the internet already had this fight more than 25 years ago, and the customers won.

Then in 2012, the Wall Street Journal reported that Orbitz had found Mac users spend as much as 30% more a night on hotels. So Orbitz started showing Mac users different, sometimes more expensive hotel options than Windows users.

Orbitz said it wasn't charging different people different prices for the same room. It was changing which hotels it showed you first. And you could still sort by price.

But you can see where this is going. Amazon in 2000 was random. Orbitz in 2012 used one fact about you: the computer you were on. So what happens when a company has a thousand facts about you?

## CHAPTER 3: WHAT THE GOVERNMENT FOUND

In July 2024, the Federal Trade Commission ordered eight companies to hand over information. These weren't stores. They were the middlemen, the firms that sell pricing technology to stores.

[VISUAL: FTC seal, "Surveillance Pricing 6(b) Study"]

The FTC called the practice "surveillance pricing." That's when companies, quote, "adjust the prices of goods and services for individual consumers," using data about them.

And on January 17, 2025, the FTC released its first findings. According to the law firm McCarter & English, which summarized them, here's some of what these tools can use:

Your location. Your device type and browser. Your age and gender. What you bought before. What you left sitting in your cart. And even micro-interactions, like how you move your mouse and how far you scroll down a page.

[VISUAL: Checklist animating — location, device, age, past purchases, abandoned cart, mouse movement]

The FTC even gave a hypothetical example. Say a company figures out you're a new parent because you've been buying baby stuff. Then it sees recent searches that suggest your baby is sick. So it offers you fast delivery on a baby thermometer, at a premium price.

That's the nightmare version. A company that knows you're desperate, and prices for it.

Now, to be fair, the FTC said this study was meant to gather information, not to accuse any company of breaking the law. And it was released in a 3-to-2 vote along party lines. The two Republican commissioners, Andrew Ferguson and Melissa Holyoak, objected to how and when it was released. They said the outgoing Democratic majority was "slowly dripping out information" instead of finishing a proper report.

Then things changed fast. Days later, Ferguson became FTC chair. And within a week of the FTC asking the public to comment on surveillance pricing, that comment period, which was supposed to stay open until April, was shut down.

[VISUAL: Retail Brew headline, Jan. 24, 2025]

So by early 2025, it looked like the federal investigation into this stuff was basically dead.

It wasn't.

## CHAPTER 4: IT'S ALREADY ON YOUR SCREEN

While Washington was fighting about it, New York did something different. It passed a law that doesn't ban anything. It just makes companies tell you.

It's called the Algorithmic Pricing Disclosure Act. If a company uses your personal data to set a price with an algorithm, it has to show you this exact sentence:

[VISUAL: Full screen, all caps: "THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA."]

"THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA."

Retailers sued to block it. They lost. On October 8, 2025, Judge Jed Rakoff in federal court in Manhattan dismissed the lawsuit.

And in November 2025, according to Business Insider, that sentence started showing up on people's phones in New York. DoorDash showed it as a pop-up. Uber Eats showed it before checkout.

[VISUAL: Mock-up of app pop-up (recreated, not a real screenshot)]

Now, what did those companies say they were using? DoorDash said it uses things like your delivery address and past orders to set fees and offer personalized promotions. Uber Eats said, "Your location is used to help us calculate fees and savings."

So it might be pretty ordinary stuff, like how far away you live. But here's the catch. The law makes them say they're doing it. It doesn't make them explain how much it changed your price.

And it's not just food apps.

**Airlines.** In July 2025, Delta told investors that an AI company called Fetcherr was setting prices on about 3% of its domestic network, and it wanted to get to 20% by the end of the year. Delta's president, Glen Hauenstein, said, "We like what we see. We like it a lot."

Three senators, Ruben Gallego, Richard Blumenthal, and Mark Warner, wrote to Delta warning about fares rising "up to each individual customer's personal 'pain point.'" Delta pushed back hard. It said the system is based on things like route demand and fuel prices, and that it has no fare product that "targets customers with individualized offers." Fetcherr said its technology doesn't use personally identifiable information.

**Grocery stores.** Walmart plans to put digital price tags in all of its U.S. stores by the end of 2026, according to CNBC. Digital tags can change prices instantly, which made a lot of people nervous. Walmart's U.S. chief operating officer, Kieran Shanahan, said surge pricing is "not our approach," and that the labels are "absolutely not collecting people's personal information."

And in 2024, Senators Elizabeth Warren and Bob Casey went after Kroger over a partnership with Microsoft involving cameras in aisles and personalized deals based on things like age and gender. Kroger told the AP its digital labels aren't linked to facial recognition and don't surge prices based on demand.

[VISUAL: Digital shelf label close-up]

So every company says the same thing. We're not doing it. And maybe they're not. But you'll notice the pattern. The tools all exist. And one of the clearest looks we've ever gotten at how this works came from volunteers on a video call, checking the price of eggs.

## CHAPTER 5: THE WEEK INSTACART FOLDED

Let's go back to Instacart, because December 2025 was a brutal month for them.

[VISUAL: Calendar, December 2025]

December 9: the Consumer Reports and Groundwork investigation comes out.

On December 17, Reuters reported that the FTC, the same agency that looked like it had given up on surveillance pricing, had sent Instacart what's called a civil investigative demand. That's basically a legal order to hand over information, in this case about its pricing.

Around the same time, in a completely separate case, Instacart agreed to pay $60 million in refunds to settle an FTC lawsuit. That one was about other things. The FTC alleged Instacart advertised "free delivery" while charging service fees and made refunds hard to get. Instacart said it "flatly" denies any wrongdoing.

And then, on December 22, Instacart pulled the plug. It announced it was ending the Eversight price tests, and that retailers would no longer have access to the tool.

In a blog post, Instacart said, "That's not okay – especially for a company built on trust, transparency, and affordability." It still rejected the idea that this was "price surveillance," and repeated that its tests never used personal data.

[VISUAL: Instacart blog post headline]

Consumer Reports CEO Phil Radford welcomed the decision to "end its secret, AI-driven pricing experiments on unsuspecting shoppers."

So that's a win, right? Story over?

Not even close. Because Instacart stopping didn't make the technology go away. And that's when the states started moving.

## CHAPTER 6: THE STATES FIGHT BACK

[VISUAL: U.S. map lighting up state by state]

**Maryland went first.** On April 28, 2026, Governor Wes Moore signed the Protection From Predatory Pricing Act. It bans grocery stores of at least 15,000 square feet, and food delivery apps, from using surveillance pricing to charge individual shoppers more for food. Fines go up to $10,000 per violation, and up to $25,000 for repeat offenders.

It took effect on October 1, 2026.

**Connecticut** signed a ban in June.

**New Jersey** signed one on July 23. Governor Mikie Sherrill signed it at a grocery store in Newark, and said, "Part of affordability is making sure consumers get a fair shake." New Jersey also paused the installation of new digital price tags for a year while the state studies them.

**New York** went the furthest. In June, the legislature passed the One Fair Price Act, which would ban using things like your browsing history, location, income, or ZIP code to set individual prices. As of early October, it was still waiting on Governor Kathy Hochul's signature or veto. Business groups have asked her to change it first.

And then this became one of the rare things both parties in Washington agree on. On August 4, 2026, the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime and Counterterrorism, chaired by Republican Josh Hawley, held a hearing literally called "Your Data, Their Profit." Democrat Richard Blumenthal said, "We need a law. We need a federal law."

[VISUAL: Hawley and Blumenthal at the hearing]

But there is a real other side to this, and it's worth hearing out.

The National Retail Federation, the big retail trade group, says these tools are mostly used to respond to competitors and demand, and to give people personalized discounts. And it argues that competition among more than 600,000 retailers pushes stores to keep prices affordable and keep shoppers' trust.

In New Jersey, Michele Siekerka, president of the New Jersey Business & Industry Association, warned that "the unintended consequences could very well be the elimination of some loyalty programs, coupons, and other customer-specific discounts."

And at the Senate hearing, Wharton marketing professor Z. John Zhang argued that personalized pricing can sometimes help consumers, especially price-sensitive ones. Think student discounts or senior pricing. That's technically charging different people different prices too.

And to be fair to that point, Maryland's law and New York's bill both carve out exceptions for things like discounts, loyalty programs, and coupons. Connecticut's law has an exception for discounts too.

So the fight isn't really about whether different people can ever pay different prices. It's about whether a company can quietly raise your price because of what it knows about you.

## ENDING: WHY IT MATTERS

[VISUAL: Old-school paper price tag]

For most of history, a price was public. It was on a sticker. Everyone in the store saw the same number. If it was too high, you could see it, you could complain about it, and you could go across the street.

That's the whole reason competition works. You can only shop around against a price you can see.

What those 40 people on that video call proved is that the price on your phone isn't always a fact. Sometimes it's a guess about you. A test. A number picked to see if you'll flinch.

Instacart stopped. Amazon stopped more than 25 years ago. But the tools have only gotten better since then, and the data has only gotten bigger. And right now, whether a company is allowed to use what it knows about you to set your price depends mostly on which state you live in.

So here's my advice, and it's not financial advice, just common sense. If something feels expensive in an app, check it somewhere else. Check the store. Check another app. Ask a friend what they see. Because a big reason any of this came out is that regular people compared their screens.

Your price isn't my price. And until the law catches up, the only way you'll know is if we compare notes.
