It's 1921. A Canadian army officer named James Sutherland Brown — his friends call him "Buster" — is driving around the northern United States in civilian clothes. He's checking roads. Bridges. Rail lines.
Because Buster has a plan. It's called Defence Scheme No. 1. And it says that if war ever breaks out between the United States and the British Empire, Canada should not wait to be invaded.
Canada should invade first.
Seattle. Great Falls, Montana. Minneapolis. Albany. Grab them fast, then fall back and wait for Britain to show up.
Now, I know what you're thinking. Canada? The country famous for apologizing to furniture? But here's the thing: by 1921, Canadians had very good reasons to be nervous about their neighbor. Americans had invaded them before. Twice. And plenty of Americans had said, out loud, that the whole continent should be one country.
So how did a cold, thinly populated patchwork of colonies, right next to the most powerful nation on Earth, end up as the second-largest country in the world — and never get swallowed?
The answer isn't a war. It's a pattern. Every time Canada was about to break apart or be absorbed, somebody sat down at a table and made a deal. Nobody loved the deal. Everybody signed it anyway.
And the real story — the part school skipped — is who wasn't invited to the table.

Let's start at the very beginning, which, for once, is actually hard to date.
During the last Ice Age, most of what's now Canada was buried under ice sheets. But Siberia and Alaska were connected by dry land, and people walked across.
When? Historians still argue about this — loudly. The mainstream view puts the first big wave of people into North America about 14,000 years ago. But at a site in the northern Yukon called Bluefish Caves, researchers Lauriane Bourgeon, Ariane Burke and Thomas Higham dated cut marks on animal bones to about 24,000 years ago. Some archaeologists think that's the earliest human presence on the continent. Others aren't convinced the marks were made by humans at all. Many Indigenous oral traditions say something simpler: we have been here since time immemorial.
What nobody debates is what happened next. Over thousands of years, people spread across the whole northern half of the continent and built hundreds of distinct nations. The Haudenosaunee in the east, with farms and longhouses. The Cree across the woodlands and plains. The Inuit in the Arctic, thriving in a place where your phone battery would die in four minutes.
Keep that in mind, because every map you're about to see draws lines over people who were already there.
Fast-forward to about a thousand years ago. Vikings sail over from Greenland and set up camp on the northern tip of Newfoundland, at a place now called L'Anse aux Meadows.
And here's the coolest dating trick in this whole video. Back in 992, a massive solar storm left a radiocarbon spike in tree rings all over the world. In 2021, a team led by Margot Kuitems and Michael Dee found wood at the site that had been cut with metal tools — and counted the rings from that spike out to the bark. Three different trees. Same answer. They were cut in the year 1021.
That's 471 years before Columbus. So no, Columbus was not the first European in the Americas. He wasn't even close.
The Vikings didn't stay. Which means our first European colony in Canada lasted roughly as long as a gym membership.
Next up: France.
In 1535, on his second voyage, a French explorer named Jacques Cartier — that's zhahk kar-tee-AY — sails up the giant river we now call the St. Lawrence. On board are two young men from the village of Stadacona, where Quebec City stands today. Cartier had taken them to France after his first trip, and now they're guiding him home. They tell him the route to "kanata."
In their Iroquoian language, kanata meant village. They were talking about their village.
Cartier, though, started using the word for the village, the land around it, and everything ruled by its chief, Donnacona. Within about a decade, European maps were labeling everything north of the river "Canada."
So the second-largest country on Earth is named, essentially, "Village." Which honestly is very on-brand.
Now, why would anyone sail across an ocean to freeze? One word: hats.
In Europe, the hottest fashion item was felt hats, and the best felt came from beaver fur. So the early history of Canada is, at its core, a story about beavers and the people who traded them.
France builds a colony along the St. Lawrence, called New France. It's small, Catholic and French-speaking, and it lives on the fur trade with Indigenous partners who know the land, the rivers and the animals.
Then England joins the game. On May 2, 1670, King Charles II gives a charter to a group of investors called "the Governor and Company of Adventurers of England trading into Hudson's Bay." They get the sole right to trade across every piece of land whose rivers drain into Hudson Bay. They name it Rupert's Land, after the king's cousin, Prince Rupert.
Pause on that. A king in London, who never set foot there, hands a private company a chunk of a continent bigger than any European country — land full of people who'd never heard of him.
That company lasted 355 years. It became a department store chain. Hold onto it. It comes back, with a price tag.
So now you've got two empires in North America, France and Britain, and they hate each other the way only two neighbors with the same hobby can. In the 1750s, that turns into a world war. Americans call it the French and Indian War. Everyone else calls it the Seven Years' War.
The key moment comes at Quebec City in 1759. The city sits on top of cliffs. The British commander, James Wolfe, has spent all summer failing to get at it. So on the night of September 12th, his troops sneak up a narrow path in the dark and, by morning, about 4,400 British soldiers are standing in a field outside the walls, on land once owned by a farmer named Abraham Martin. The Plains of Abraham.
The French commander, the Marquis de Montcalm, rushes out to fight instead of waiting behind the walls.
The battle itself is short — often described as lasting well under an hour. Wolfe is shot and dies on the field. Montcalm is wounded during the retreat and dies the next morning. Both generals, gone, in one morning. Each side took roughly 650 casualties. Five days later, Quebec surrenders. In 1763, France signs New France over to Britain.
And now Britain has a problem. It owns a colony full of French-speaking Catholics who just lost a war to it. And next door are thirteen British colonies getting very loud about taxes.
So Britain does something surprising. In 1774, it passes the Quebec Act. It lets the people of Quebec keep their Catholic faith openly and keep French civil law.
Basically: "You can keep being you. Just don't join the guys down south."
And Canada did what Canada does. It made a deal.
The Thirteen Colonies hate it. They're Protestant, they're angry, and they're about to start a revolution. In 1775, the new Continental Congress approves an invasion of Canada. The plan: march north, take Quebec, and invite the locals to join the revolution.
At first it works. American troops take Montreal in November 1775 without a fight. Then, on the last night of the year, General Richard Montgomery and Benedict Arnold attack Quebec City in a blizzard. Yes — that Benedict Arnold, before he switched sides.
It goes badly. Montgomery is killed in the first volley. By the next spring, the Americans retreat. Most of the people of Quebec, it turns out, didn't want to join. The deal held.
Then, when the Revolution ends in 1783, something happens that shapes Canada forever. Tens of thousands of colonists who stayed loyal to Britain — the Loyalists — flee north. Farmers, merchants, Haudenosaunee allies who got land along the Grand River, and about 3,500 Black Loyalists, many of whom had won their freedom by fighting for Britain, and who then faced serious discrimination in the Maritimes.
So English-speaking Canada was, in large part, founded by Americans who lost an argument.
Round two. The War of 1812. Britain and the United States are fighting over a whole list of things, and conquering Canada is one of the openly stated American goals.
In April 1813, American troops capture York — the little capital of Upper Canada, which you know today as Toronto — and burn government buildings.
The next year, British forces march into Washington, D.C. and set fire to the Capitol and the White House, partly in retaliation for York.
Now, you've probably heard that Canada burned down the White House. It's a fun bar fact. It's not quite true. It was British troops under British command. Canada didn't exist as a country yet. Canadians just like to take partial credit, the way your cousin takes credit for introducing your parents because he was at the party.
The war ends in a draw, more or less. Borders stay where they were. But the colonists in Canada come out of it with a story about the time they held off the Americans. Remember that feeling. It's about to become a country.
By the 1860s, British North America is a set of separate colonies. The biggest, the Province of Canada, is actually two colonies glued together — one English, one French — and they can barely govern. Parliament is permanently stuck.
And then come the Fenians. They're Irish nationalists based in the United States, many of them Civil War veterans, and their plan goes like this: invade Canada, hold it, and trade it back to Britain for Irish independence. Between 1866 and 1871, they try several times. Most of the raids flop. But at Ridgeway, near Niagara, in June 1866, they beat a Canadian militia force.
How much did that push the colonies together? Historians still argue about this. Some say the Fenian scare helped swing New Brunswick's 1866 election toward union. Others say the deal was already basically done. What's clear is that the colonies felt small, divided and very close to a giant, fully armed neighbor that had just finished a civil war.
So they sit down at the table. The leading figure is John A. Macdonald — a lawyer, a brilliant political dealmaker, and, by many accounts, a heavy drinker. On July 1, 1867, under a British law, the Province of Canada — now split into Ontario and Quebec — plus Nova Scotia and New Brunswick become "one Dominion under the name of Canada."
And Canada did what Canada does. It made a deal.
Here's the thing, though. That first Canada was small. Four provinces hugging the St. Lawrence and the Atlantic. Everything west of Lake Superior? Still Rupert's Land. Still owned by that fur company.
So Canada goes shopping. In the late 1860s, the Hudson's Bay Company hands Rupert's Land back to the British Crown, which passes it to Canada. Canada pays £300,000, plus millions of acres of land for the company to keep.
It's the largest land purchase in Canadian history.
And Canada did what Canada does. It made a deal.
There's just one problem. Nobody asked the roughly 11,000 people actually living at the Red River settlement — mostly Métis, people of mixed First Nations and European, mostly French, ancestry, with their own language, their own farms and their own way of life.
Now where were we? Oh yeah. The French and English signed a deal, Canada was born, and then it bought half a continent from a fur company — without asking the people who lived there.
Those people had a leader. His name was Louis Riel — that's loo-EE ree-EL. He was 25, Métis, educated in Montreal, and absolutely not in the mood.
In 1869, Riel and the Métis set up their own provisional government and blocked Canada from taking over. And it works. Canada is forced to negotiate a second set of terms — this time with the people of Red River. In 1870, the Manitoba Act creates the province of Manitoba, with protections for land, language and religion.
And Canada did what Canada does. It made a deal. Except this time, somebody forced their way to the table.
But that's not where Riel's story ends. During the resistance, his provisional government executed a man named Thomas Scott, and that made Riel a hated figure in English Canada. When troops arrived in 1870, he fled. He was elected to Parliament and expelled. He spent time in asylums in Quebec and lived in the United States.
Fifteen years later, the Métis and some Cree communities in what's now Saskatchewan are again losing their land and being ignored. They call Riel back. In 1885 there's armed conflict — the North-West Resistance. Canada sends troops west, fast, on a brand-new railway. The Métis are defeated at Batoche in May. Riel surrenders. He's tried for high treason and convicted, even though questions about his sanity were raised at the time.
On November 7, 1885, a railway director drives the last spike of the Canadian Pacific Railway into the ground in the mountains of British Columbia. The country is finally connected, coast to coast.
Nine days later, on November 16, Louis Riel is hanged in Regina.
To many in English Canada, he was a traitor. To French Canada, his execution was an outrage. Today, he is honored as the founder of Manitoba. Whether he's a rebel or a father of Confederation depends on who you ask — and that argument is the history of Canada in one person.
That railway was the thing that held the new country together. And the people who built some of its most dangerous sections, through the mountains of British Columbia, were thousands of workers from China.
And as soon as the railway was nearly done, in 1885, Canada passed a law charging every Chinese immigrant a $50 head tax to enter the country. No other group had to pay it. In 1903 it went up to $500 — roughly two years of a laborer's wages. Families were split for decades because men couldn't afford to bring their wives and children over. Then on July 1, 1923 — Canada's national holiday — Parliament banned almost all Chinese immigration outright. Many Chinese Canadians called that day Humiliation Day. The ban lasted until 1947. Canada formally apologized in 2006.
And at the same time, in the West, the government was pushing First Nations onto reserves under treaties that were often broken — and it was building another system.
Starting in the 1880s, the federal government, working with churches, ran residential schools. Indigenous children — more than 150,000 First Nations, Métis and Inuit children over the life of the system — were taken from their families and sent to schools whose main purpose was to cut their ties to their language, their culture and their families. Many were abused. Thousands died, mostly from disease and malnutrition; the exact number isn't known because the schools kept poor records. The last school closed in 1996. Not 1896. 1996.
In 2015, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission called the system cultural genocide.
These were the people who were never at the table. And the deal was paid for, in part, by them.
In 1914, when Britain went to war, Canada was automatically at war too. It didn't get to choose.
But in April 1917, at a ridge in northern France called Vimy, something changed. For the first time, all four Canadian divisions attacked together, men from every part of the country side by side. The French had already lost more than 100,000 killed and wounded trying to take that ridge. On Easter Monday, April 9, at 5:30 in the morning, in sleet and snow, the Canadians went over.
By April 12, the ridge was theirs. 3,598 Canadians were killed and about 7,000 wounded.
Across the whole war, about 61,000 Canadians died — from a country of just over 8 million people.
You'll often hear that Vimy was "the birth of a nation." That's partly how Canadians came to remember it, decades later — historians debate how much it mattered at the time versus how much it grew in memory. What's clear is that Canada came out of that war feeling less like a colony and more like a country. In the 1920s Canada got its own embassy in Washington, and over the next decade Britain stopped making laws for it.
And that's the moment our cold open happens. 1921. Buster Brown, in his civilian clothes, scouting the northern US.
So yes. Defence Scheme No. 1 was real. Written April 12, 1921. The idea: hit the US first, grab key cities, then fall back and wait for help. The problem: Brown never checked with Britain — which had no plans to send a big army to defend Canada at all. The plan was shelved in 1928.
And in 1930 the US Army drew up its own plan to invade Canada, in case of war with Britain. It was called War Plan Red.
Neither plan was ever used. Within twenty years, the two countries were the closest allies on Earth. But the fact that both plans existed tells you everything about how that border felt in the 1920s.
Canada fought hard again in the Second World War. And at home, it did something it would later apologize for.
After Pearl Harbor, starting in January 1942, the government removed about 21,000 to 22,000 Japanese Canadians from the West Coast — more than 90 percent of the community, most of them Canadian-born or naturalized. Their homes, boats and businesses were taken and sold. Not a single Japanese Canadian was ever charged with wrongdoing. They weren't allowed back to the coast until 1949. In 1988, Prime Minister Brian Mulroney apologized, and survivors received $21,000 each.
After the war, the deal-making speeds up.
Newfoundland had been its own dominion for decades, then went broke in the Depression and was run by a British-appointed commission. In 1948, it votes twice. The second time, joining Canada wins with 52.34 percent. And on March 31, 1949, Newfoundland becomes Canada's tenth province.
And Canada did what Canada does. It made a deal. By four and a half points.
Then, in 1964, Parliament spends months fighting over a new flag — a debate so nasty one historian called it among the ugliest in the history of the House of Commons. The final vote happens at 2:15 in the morning. On February 15, 1965, the red maple leaf goes up on Parliament Hill for the first time. Canada is 98 years old and finally gets a flag of its own.
But the oldest tension in the country — French and English — never went away. In 1982, Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau finally brings Canada's constitution home from Britain and adds a Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Queen Elizabeth II signs it in Ottawa on April 17, 1982.
Nine provinces sign on. One doesn't. Quebec's premier, René Lévesque — ruh-NAY lay-VEK — refuses, saying Quebec was shut out of the final deal. Quebec is still legally bound by it. Quebec's government has never formally endorsed it.
And Canada did what Canada does. It made a deal. This time, without Quebec.
Which brings us to the night Canada almost stopped existing.
October 30, 1995. Quebec holds a referendum on whether to become a sovereign country. Turnout is about 93 percent — the highest in the province's history.
All night, the numbers swing. Then the final count: No — stay in Canada — 50.58 percent. Yes — 49.42 percent.
The country survived by 54,288 votes. That's fewer people than fit in the stadium at a big college football game.
No new deal came out of that night. Just the realization that the old one was held together with tape. Whether Quebec should be a country is still a live question for some Quebecers today, and that's a debate for them, not for us.
Four years later, in 1999, Canada makes another deal — this time with the Inuit, who negotiated the creation of an entire territory, Nunavut, where they make up most of the population. A deal where the people affected were actually at the table.
So, back to our question. How did a few cold colonies next to the most powerful country on Earth end up as the second-largest country in the world and never get absorbed?
Not by winning wars. By making deals. The Quebec Act. Confederation. The Manitoba Act. Newfoundland. The constitution. Seven deals, give or take, and every one of them was a compromise that someone hated.
But the story of those deals isn't just about who signed them. It's about who wasn't asked. The Métis at Red River. Chinese railway workers. Japanese Canadian fishermen. More than 150,000 Indigenous children. A lot of modern Canada is the slow, unfinished work of pulling up more chairs.
Buster Brown spent 1921 sneaking around the United States planning a war. Today, the border he scouted is crossed by enormous volumes of trade every single day. The plan never happened.
