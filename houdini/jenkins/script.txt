It's 1713. Europe has just finished a long war over who gets to sit on Spain's throne. The peace deal is called the Treaty of Utrecht, and buried inside it is a prize Britain has wanted for years.
Spain's empire in the Americas is enormous. It runs from Florida and what's now Mexico all the way down through most of South America. And Spain runs it like a store that's only allowed one supplier. Goods going in come from Spanish merchants, on Spanish ships, through Spanish ports. Everyone else is locked out.
Utrecht cracks the door open for Britain in two places.
The first is a contract called the asiento de negros. It gives Britain the exclusive right to deliver enslaved Africans to Spain's colonies: 4,800 people a year, for thirty years.
The second is a single ship. Once a year, one British vessel may sell ordinary goods at Spain's great trade fairs, in places like Porto Bello in Panama and Veracruz in Mexico.
Both rights go to a British business called the South Sea Company.
Let's be plain about what that first contract means. The 4,800 are people. They're taken from West Africa, chained below deck for weeks, and sold in ports like Cartagena, Havana and Veracruz. The company never managed to deliver its full quota. But over the next quarter century, tens of thousands of people were carried into Spanish America under this agreement. This is the trade at the center of our story. Everything that follows, every insult and every battle, sits on top of it.
And here's the catch. The contract is a door, and once a door is open, a lot of things can walk through it.
British merchants, many of them sailing out of Jamaica, start smuggling. Cloth, tools, all kinds of goods, slipped into Spanish ports without paying Spanish taxes. The legal trade is small. One historian calls the asiento a "commercial illusion." The smuggling is where the money is.
