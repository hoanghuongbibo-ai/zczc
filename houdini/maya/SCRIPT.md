### BEAT 2 — The World Before (c. 1000 BC)
[MAP: Maya region — southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, western Honduras; label modern borders as "today"]
Rewind three thousand years. You're living in what's now the Mexican state of Tabasco, in the lowlands near today's border with Guatemala. Hot, wet, green. For generations, your people have moved between camps, hunting, fishing, gathering. But lately you've been planting something. A skinny, stubborn grass that, with enough patience, turns into corn.
And corn changes things. Corn means you can stay.
So one season, you and a lot of your neighbors start carrying dirt. Basket after basket, for years. And what you build is enormous: a raised earthen platform nearly a mile long — about 4,600 feet — and 30 to 50 feet high, with nine wide causeways running out from it like spokes on a wheel.
[VISUAL: animated lidar sweep reveals Aguada Fénix out of flat ranchland]
This place is called Aguada Fénix, and here's the strange part. It's so wide and so flat that if you walk across it today, it just looks like the landscape. Cattle graze on it. Nobody spotted it until archaeologists flew over with laser scanners and published it in 2020. It's the oldest monumental building ever found in the Maya area, built somewhere between 1000 and 800 BC — and it's the biggest, too. Bigger than any pyramid the Maya ever built afterward.
And notice what's missing. No giant carved heads. No thrones. No portrait of a king. The team that dug it, led by Takeshi Inomata, thinks this was communal work — lots of people building together, not a ruler ordering them around.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Interpretation — Inomata et al., Nature 2020]
That's an interpretation, and archaeologists still argue about how much the Maya learned from their older neighbors, the Olmec, versus how much they invented on their own. But hold on to that image. The very first big thing the Maya made was made by everybody.
Because that's about to change.

### BEAT 3 — The Spark: Kings, and Ending Number One
Over the next thousand years, your great-great-great-grandchildren build cities. Real ones — plazas, temples, painted walls, carved stone. And somewhere along the way, a new kind of person shows up at the top: a holy lord. A king who says he speaks to the gods, and that the rain and the corn depend on him. [VISUAL: Time-lapse of Maya villages growing into stone cities, culminating in a holy king performing a rain ritual atop a temple]
Now, here's the first thing school usually gets wrong. There was never one Maya empire. No single capital, no emperor. Think less "Roman Empire" and more "the NFL." Dozens of franchises. Same language family, same gods, same calendar, same ballgame. And every one of them trying to beat the others.
[VISUAL: map dotted with dozens of city-state icons]
Some of the earliest giants rose in what's now northern Guatemala. Cities like El Mirador, with temple platforms that still poke up out of the rainforest canopy today. And then, around the second century AD, several of those early giants were largely abandoned. Why? Historians still argue — drought, war, exhausted land. Probably some mix.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Ending #1 — the Preclassic collapse, c. 2nd century AD]
The big cities emptied. But the people didn't vanish. They moved, they farmed, they rebuilt somewhere else.
The kings fell. The farmers stayed.
That's ending number one. Let's keep counting.

### BEAT 4 — The Escalation Chain
#### 4a. The Stranger (AD 378)
[MUSIC: tension]
It's January, AD 378. You're a scribe in Tikal, one of the most powerful cities in the Maya world, in what's now northern Guatemala. Pyramids so tall they'll one day stick up above the jungle like the masts of buried ships. [VISUAL: Maya scribe carving glyphs onto bark paper in Tikal, with towering pyramids rising above the surrounding jungle]
And today, someone arrives.
The carved stones of Tikal and its neighbors give him a name: Siyaj K'ak' — "Fire Is Born." [PRONUNCIATION: SEE-yahk KAHK]. And they record something chilling. On the very day he arrived, Tikal's long-reigning king, Chak Tok Ich'aak [chahk tohk ee-CH'AHK], died.
[ARTIFACT: Tikal Stela 31; Tikal "Marcador"]
Within about a year, a new king sits on Tikal's throne — the son of a mysterious foreign lord the Maya called "Spearthrower Owl." And suddenly Tikal's art is full of warriors in the style of Teotihuacan, the giant city near today's Mexico City, roughly 600 miles away. That's about the drive from New York to Detroit, except on foot, through mountains and rainforest.
[MAP: arrow Teotihuacan → Tikal]
So what was this? A takeover, a dynastic coup, or a powerful alliance? Here's where the experts stand. Epigrapher David Stuart reads the inscriptions as describing a military conquest that installed a new political order. Others have argued for something more like an alliance. What nobody disputes is the result: for centuries afterward, Maya kings kept bragging about their connection to that "arrival."
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Debated — conquest or alliance?]
The point is, from here on, being a Maya king isn't just about rain. It's about rivals. And the rivalry is about to get personal.

#### 4b. The King Who Lost His Head (AD 738)
Jump ahead to the 700s. You're in Copán, in what's now western Honduras — the southernmost of the great Maya cities. Your king is Waxaklajuun Ub'aah K'awiil [wah-shahk-lah-HOON oo-BAH kah-WEEL], though modern scholars, struggling with the glyphs, nicknamed him "18 Rabbit."
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Waxaklajuun Ub'aah K'awiil — "18 Rabbit" (r. 695–738)]
18 Rabbit has ruled for more than forty years. He's filled the city with towering stone portraits of himself. Years earlier, he even oversaw the installation of a new ruler in Quiriguá, a smaller town that's been under Copán's thumb — a guy named K'ak' Tiliw Chan Yopaat [KAHK tee-LEEW chahn yoh-PAHT]. [VISUAL: Stone statues of 18 Rabbit towering over Copán, then cut to K'ak' Tiliw Chan Yopaat being crowned ruler of Quiriguá]
In January of 738, 18 Rabbit dedicates the final version of his grand ballcourt. It's his masterpiece.
A hundred and thirteen days later, he's dead. Captured and beheaded at Quiriguá — by the man he'd helped put in power.
[MUSIC: drop]
The inscriptions at Quiriguá celebrate it for decades. How did a small town pull this off? Some scholars think the superpower Calakmul was quietly backing Quiriguá. That's possible — but it's a reconstruction from incomplete evidence, not a confirmed fact.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Calakmul's role — debated]
What it shows is the world the Maya kings had built. A world where every alliance could flip, every vassal was a threat, and your carved stone portrait could outlive you by a very short margin.

#### 4c. The Boy King (AD 615–683)
Not every king ends like that, though. Head west to Palenque, in today's Chiapas, Mexico. In 615, the city is recovering from attacks, and the throne goes to a twelve-year-old boy: K'inich Janaab' Pakal [kee-NEECH hah-NAHB pah-KAHL]. [VISUAL: A damaged Maya city slowly rebuilding, as a twelve-year-old is crowned king]
Imagine being a sixth-grader and getting handed a kingdom.
Pakal rules for sixty-eight years. According to the inscriptions, he dies in 683 at about eighty. In that time, he turns Palenque into one of the most beautiful cities the Maya ever built, and he plans his own grave inside a pyramid we now call the Temple of the Inscriptions. [VISUAL: Time-lapse of Palenque flourishing under Pakal, ending with workers constructing his burial chamber inside the Temple of the Inscriptions]
Then he's forgotten — sort of — for twelve and a half centuries.
In 1948, a Mexican archaeologist named Alberto Ruz Lhuillier [roos loo-YEE-ay] notices that one floor slab in that temple has holes in it, like it was meant to be lifted. Under it: a staircase packed with rubble. It takes four seasons of digging to clear it. And in 1952, at the bottom, he finds a sealed tomb with a carved stone sarcophagus and the remains of the king.
[ARTIFACT: Pakal's sarcophagus lid; jade mosaic mask — National Museum of Anthropology, Mexico City]
Here's a fun wrinkle: the first scientists to study the skeleton thought it looked like a man in his forties, not eighties. Which would mean either the bones or the inscriptions were wrong. Later teams re-examined the remains, and today it's generally accepted that this is Pakal. But it's a good reminder that even bones and stone can argue with each other.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Skeletal age vs inscriptions — once debated]

#### 4d. The Peak — and How Big It Really Was
Now zoom out. It's around the year 750, the height of what archaeologists call the Late Classic period. How many people are living in the Maya lowlands?
For most of the twentieth century, the answer was "not that many." The jungle looked empty. A few ceremonial centers, scattered villages.
Then in 2016, a project called the PACUNAM Lidar Initiative flew planes with laser scanners over about 830 square miles of northern Guatemala. The lasers slip between the leaves, bounce off the ground, and the computer strips the forest away.
[VISUAL: canopy dissolves into a lidar map: houses, causeways, terraces, canals, fortifications]
They counted 61,480 structures. Houses. Palaces. Roads. Farm terraces. Defensive walls. When the team, led by Marcello Canuto and Francisco Estrada-Belli, extrapolated across the whole central lowlands — an area about the size of Indiana — they estimated somewhere between 7 and 11 million people at the peak.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: 7–11 million — an estimate, Canuto et al., Science 2018]
That's an extrapolation, not a headcount, and it has critics. But the direction is clear: this wasn't a few temples lost in the jungle. It was a crowded, engineered landscape. Millions of people living off corn — and corn lives off rain.
Remember that. It's about to matter more than anything else.

### BEAT 6 — The Twist: The Stones Were Talking
[MUSIC: curious]
For a long time, the experts had a lovely, peaceful picture of the Maya. Gentle priest-astronomers, staring at the stars, carving calendar math into stone. No wars to speak of. No politics. Just very calm people who were very into dates. [VISUAL: Maya priests studying the stars and carving calendar glyphs into stone, with a peaceful city in the background]
And the carvings? Mostly unreadable. Scholars could do the numbers, but the words were a locked door.
Then two outsiders picked the lock.
In 1952, a young Soviet scholar named Yuri Knorozov [yoo-ree kno-ROH-zof] published a paper arguing that Maya glyphs weren't just pictures for ideas. Many of them stood for sounds — syllables. You could sound them out. It was the Cold War, and a lot of Western experts brushed him off.
Meanwhile, Tatiana Proskouriakoff [pros-koor-YAH-kof] — born in Siberia, raised in Pennsylvania, trained as an architect, and at first doing archaeology for no salary — was staring at a set of stone monuments from a site called Piedras Negras in Guatemala. She noticed that the dates on them came in clusters that fit one human lifespan. A birth. A coronation. Anniversaries. Death.
In 1960, she published her conclusion. These weren't astronomy charts. They were the life stories of real kings.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Proskouriakoff, "Historical Implications of a Pattern of Dates at Piedras Negras, Guatemala," American Antiquity, 1960]
So the gentle stargazers? Gone. What came out of the stone was something much more human: kings boasting, capturing rivals, marrying for alliances, holding grudges for generations. The stones were basically press releases. Some of them were trash talk. [VISUAL: Maya stone inscriptions reveal kings boasting of victories, capturing rivals, and insulting rival rulers]
That's how we know about Fire Is Born. That's how we know 18 Rabbit lost his head. That's how Pakal got his name back.
And it's how we know what happened next. Because sometime in the 800s, across the southern lowlands, the stones start going quiet.

### BEAT 7 — The Climax: The Long Dry (c. 800–950)
[MUSIC: slow, sparse]
It's around the year 850. You're a farmer outside one of the great southern cities. Your family has worked the same fields for generations, and every year it's gone the same way: dry season, then the rains, then corn. [VISUAL: Maya farmer tending a cornfield outside a great city, with the dry season giving way to heavy rains]
Except the rains have been late. And then light. And then, some years, barely there.
We know this now with surprising precision. In 2018, a team from the University of Cambridge and the University of Florida analyzed gypsum layers at the bottom of Lake Chichancanab [chee-chahn-kah-NAHB] in the Yucatán. During droughts, more lake water evaporates, and the water that's left gets chemically "heavier." Some of that water gets locked inside gypsum crystals as they form. Measure the crystals, and you're almost measuring the ancient water itself.
[VISUAL: cutaway of lake sediment layers, gypsum crystals glowing]
What they found: during this period, yearly rainfall dropped by somewhere between 41 and 54 percent compared with today. During the worst stretches, by as much as 70 percent.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: −41% to −54% rainfall; up to −70% at peak — Evans et al., Science 2018]
Imagine your town's water supply getting cut in half. For years. Now imagine your entire food system, and your entire government, depends on that water.
Because here's the trap. The king's whole job — the reason he gets to live in the palace and you don't — is that he says he can talk to the gods who send the rain. And the rain isn't coming.
So what happens to a king who can't deliver?
The honest answer is: we don't have one clean explanation, and historians argue about the mix. Drought is the strongest single piece of evidence. But it landed on a system that was already strained — packed with people, at war with itself, with kings competing to build bigger and fight harder. Some scholars stress environmental damage, others stress warfare, others stress trade routes shifting north. Most now see the drought as the push that tipped an already wobbly structure.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Causes debated — drought + war + overpopulation + political breakdown]
What we can see is the result. One by one, the great southern cities stop carving monuments. Stop building. Stop crowning kings. [VISUAL: Maya cities gradually abandoned, stone monuments left unfinished, temples falling silent and jungle reclaiming the plazas] The very last known Long Count date carved on any Maya monument comes from Toniná, in Chiapas, marking a calendar period that ended in January of AD 909.
[ARTIFACT: Toniná Monument 101]
After that, in the southern lowlands, the stones go silent.
[MUSIC: silence, then soft]
And the forest moves in. Roots crack the plazas. Trees climb the pyramids. Within a few generations, the cities that held tens of thousands of people are green hills. [VISUAL: Time-lapse of jungle roots cracking stone plazas as trees and vines swallow Maya pyramids, leaving only green hills beneath the forest canopy]
That's the "Maya collapse" you read about in school. That's ending number two.
But where did all those people go?
Some died — of hunger, of war. Many moved: north into the Yucatán, south into the highlands, into smaller villages that could live on less. And in the north, new powers rose. Chichén Itzá. Later, a walled city called Mayapán. Different style, same language family, same people.
The kings fell. The farmers stayed.

### BEAT 8 — Aftermath & Human Cost: The Long Conquest
[MUSIC: tonal drop — no humor from here until the legacy reset]
Fast forward to the 1500s. When the Spanish arrive, they don't find one Maya empire to topple, the way they toppled the Aztec capital. They find dozens of small, independent kingdoms. The first Spanish expedition to land on the Yucatán coast, in 1517, ended in disaster for the Spanish. [VISUAL: Spanish ships arrive on the Yucatán coast as a map reveals dozens of independent Maya kingdoms, then Spanish soldiers retreat under attack in 1517]
That fragmentation is the bitter irony of Maya history. It made them hard to conquer — the Spanish had to fight kingdom by kingdom, for decades — but it also meant there was never one army to stop them. And alongside the soldiers came something deadlier: diseases the people of the Americas had never encountered.
And then came the fire.
It's July 12th, 1562. You're in the plaza of Maní [mah-NEE], a town in the Yucatán. The Franciscan friar Diego de Landa has learned that baptized Maya are still secretly honoring their old gods. So he holds an auto-da-fé — an "act of faith" — a public ritual of punishment and penance. People are tortured. Thousands of sacred images are smashed and burned.
And so are books. Maya books — folded bark-paper codices, painted with glyphs, holding astronomy, ritual, history, medicine. Landa himself admitted to burning 27 of them. Other accounts suggest far more. [VISUAL: Painterly still of Spanish friars burning Maya bark-paper codices covered in glyphs, sacred artifacts smashed nearby as Maya townspeople watch helplessly, no gore]
He described it in his own words. Of the books, he wrote, as translated: [QUOTE] "we burned them all, which they regretted to an amazing degree." [Landa, Relación de las cosas de Yucatán, c. 1566]
Today, out of all the books the Maya ever wrote, only four pre-Columbian codices are known to survive. Four. Three are in Europe, in Dresden, Madrid and Paris — probably saved because they had already been shipped across the ocean. [VISUAL: Four surviving Maya codices displayed on a dark museum background, with three highlighted on a map in Dresden, Madrid, and Paris]
And yet — here's the strange twist in Landa's story — he also wrote one of the most important descriptions of Maya life and writing we have. The man who burned the books left behind a partial key that later helped people read them.
The conquest dragged on for over a century and a half. The very last independent Maya kingdom was the Itza [ee-TSAH] kingdom, ruling from an island city called Nojpetén [noh-peh-TEN], in Lake Petén Itzá in northern Guatemala. Its people had fled south there around the 1440s, after the fall of Mayapán.
On March 13th, 1697, Spanish forces stormed the island.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: March 13, 1697 — the last independent native kingdom in the Americas falls]
Think about that date. That's after the Pilgrims landed at Plymouth. After Harvard was founded. The last independent kingdom in the Americas fell less than eighty years before the American Revolution.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Ending #3]
That's ending number three. Surely this time it's over.
It wasn't.
In 1847, in the Yucatán, Maya communities who'd been pushed off their land and into debt rose up against the region's European-descended elite. It became known as the Caste War. At one point the rebels nearly drove their enemies off the peninsula. In the jungles of the east, they built their own independent state, centered on a town called Chan Santa Cruz, organized around a sacred "speaking cross" that gave orders through its keepers. For more than half a century, it held. [VISUAL: Map of the Yucatán showing the Caste War spreading in 1847, then Maya rebels establishing an independent state around Chan Santa Cruz and its sacred speaking cross]
Mexican troops occupied Chan Santa Cruz in 1901. Death toll estimates for the whole war vary enormously, from tens of thousands to far more.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Ending #4 — Caste War of Yucatán, 1847–1901 (estimates of deaths vary widely)]
And then the twentieth century brought the hardest chapter of all.
From 1960 to 1996, Guatemala was torn apart by an internal armed conflict between the government and leftist guerrillas. When it ended, a United Nations-backed truth commission, the Commission for Historical Clarification, investigated. Its 1999 report found that more than 200,000 people had been killed or disappeared. Eighty-three percent of the fully identified victims were Maya.
And it concluded that in the early 1980s, in specific regions, agents of the state had committed acts of genocide against Maya groups.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Commission for Historical Clarification, "Guatemala: Memory of Silence," 1999]
[MUSIC: hold silence 2 beats]
Not a collapse. Not a drought. Not an ending the Maya chose. People killed for who they were.
That's not ending number five. That's an attempt to make one.
And it failed.

### BEAT 9 — Legacy & Close
[MUSIC: slow return of warmth]
Here's where the count stops.
Today, around six million people speak a Mayan language — roughly thirty different languages, from K'iche' [kee-CHAY] in the Guatemalan highlands to Yucatec Maya on the peninsula where you bought your water. Some are thriving, taught in schools and broadcast on radio. Some are hanging by a thread. Itza', the language of that last island kingdom, is spoken today by only a small number of elders. [ON-SCREEN TEXT: 6,000,000] [VISUAL: Maya speakers in traditional communities, children learning Mayan languages in classrooms, and an elderly Itza' speaker talking in a quiet village]
And the stones are talking again. Thanks to Knorozov, Proskouriakoff and the scholars who followed, specialists can now read most surviving Maya inscriptions. The kings have their names back. 18 Rabbit. Fire Is Born. Pakal.
So let's go back to that night in 2012.
The Maya calendar never "ran out." The Long Count works like the odometer in your car. On December 21st, 2012, it rolled over to 13.0.0.0.0 — the same number it showed at the mythological start of the current era — and then it simply kept going. Out of the thousands of Maya inscriptions, only two known texts even mention that date, and neither describes the end of the world.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: 13.0.0.0.0 → 13.0.0.0.1. Nothing ended.]
The Maya didn't predict the end of the world. The rest of the world just predicted the end of the Maya. Over and over.
The first giant thing they built, three thousand years ago, was built by everybody. The kings came later. The kings carved their faces in stone and promised rain, and when the rain stopped, the kings fell. Empires came from across the ocean and burned the books, and the empires fell too. And the farmers stayed.
