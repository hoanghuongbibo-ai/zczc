### Beat 6 — The twist
So far this sounds like a story about toughness. The city gets knocked down, the city gets back up.
But here's the part that story skips. Buildings don't rebuild themselves, and toughness doesn't pay for an aqueduct. Every one of those comebacks ran on the same two things. A harbor that made this the cheapest place on the continent to do business. And a steady line of new people willing to bet on it. [VISUAL: harbor on the left, people willing to bet on the right]
So what happens when both of those stop at once?

### Beat 7 — The climax
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: 1975]
It's 1975. You're back on the corner. And this time nothing is on fire, which is what makes it frightening.
The factory jobs and the dock jobs have been leaving for twenty years. [VERIFY] Hundreds of thousands of residents have moved to the suburbs. The city has been covering the gap by borrowing, and now the lenders have had enough. [VISUAL: 1970s New York, abandoned factories and quiet docks, families moving out to the suburbs, followed by city officials reviewing financial papers as lenders refuse further loans.]
[VISUAL: 1975, New York City officials plead for federal financial aid, followed by President Gerald Ford delivering his October 29 speech at the White House.]
New York City, the financial capital of the world, is weeks from being unable to pay its own bills. So it asks Washington for help.
On October 29th, President Gerald Ford gives a speech and rules it out. "The people of this country will not be stampeded," he says.
The next morning, the Daily News runs five words across its front page.
[ARTIFACT: New York Daily News front page, October 30, 1975]
Ford to City: Drop Dead.
Ford never said that. An editor named William Brink wrote it, in pencil, on a sheet of newsprint. But the headline did what the speech couldn't. Within two months, Ford approved 2.3 billion dollars in federal loans. The city paid them back with interest. A year later Ford narrowly lost New York State, and the presidency, and some analysts think those five words are part of the reason.
The loans stop the bleeding. They don't stop the pain. The price is years of cuts to city services, and the historian Kim Phillips-Fein argues they landed hardest on the New Yorkers with the least.
Then, on a July night in 1977, lightning hits the power lines north of the city. Around 9:30, everything goes dark. It stays dark for twenty-five hours. More than sixteen hundred stores are looted or damaged. More than a thousand fires are reported. Police make 3,776 arrests, the largest mass arrest in the city's history. [VISUAL: darkened streets filled with looters, burning buildings, fire engines responding to fires, and police arresting people under flashlight beams.]
When the 1980 census comes in, the city has lost more than eight hundred thousand people in ten years. One New Yorker in ten.
New York is finished.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Count: 4]
That's four. And this is the one where people believed it.
So why wasn't it true?
Because the line of newcomers started up again. In 1970, about 1.4 million New Yorkers had been born in another country. By 2000, it was almost 2.9 million. They moved into neighborhoods everyone else had written off. That year the census counted more than eight million New Yorkers for the first time. [VISUAL: a 2000 census map showing the city's population surpassing eight million.]
