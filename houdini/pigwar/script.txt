It's just after dawn. June 15th, 1859.
You're standing in a potato patch on a small island at the top left corner of what will one day be the United States. The air smells like salt and wet cedar. Out past the trees, the water is flat and gray, and somewhere on the far side of it, seven miles away, is a British colony.
And in your potatoes, there is a pig.
It's not your pig. It belongs to the most powerful company in North America. It has been in your potatoes before. And this morning, the man who owns this patch is standing next to you, holding a rifle.
Now pull back.
Within two months, there will be hundreds of American soldiers dug in on this hillside. In the bay below you, there will be five British warships, carrying over two thousand men and around seventy guns. Two of the most powerful nations on Earth will be a single nervous trigger finger away from a war.
And it all started right here, with a pig and some potatoes.
So how does that happen? How does one dead pig put empires on the brink? And why, after all of that, does nobody fire a single shot?
To understand why anyone would bring warships to a potato patch, you have to go back thirteen years, to a piece of paper.
In the 1840s, the US and Britain both claimed a huge stretch of the Pacific Northwest. It was called the Oregon Country, and it covered what's now Oregon, Washington, Idaho and a big chunk of British Columbia. For years the two countries had basically agreed to share it, like roommates who never decided whose name goes on the lease.
But Americans were pouring in on the Oregon Trail. And in 1846, the two governments finally signed the Oregon Treaty. The deal was simple: the border would follow the 49th parallel west until it hit the water, then run to the middle of the channel which separates the continent from Vancouver's Island.
Read that again. The channel.
Here's the problem. There isn't one channel. There are two. Haro Strait runs close to Vancouver Island. Rosario Strait runs closer to the mainland. And sitting right between them is a scatter of islands, the San Juans.
If the border runs down Haro Strait, the islands are American. If it runs down Rosario Strait, they're British. And the treaty doesn't say which one.
This is the whole war. Everything else that happens is just people trying to win an argument over one sentence.
Now, these islands weren't empty. People had lived here for thousands of years.
This is the homeland of Coast Salish peoples, including the Lummi, the Songhees, the Saanich and the Samish, who fished these waters, harvested camas and shellfish, and had villages along these shores long before any treaty was written. In the entire argument that's about to unfold, between two empires over who owns this island, nobody asks them.
The British got there first in the official sense. In 1853, the Hudson's Bay Company, the giant British fur-trading business that basically ran this corner of the continent, set up a sheep farm on the south end of San Juan Island. They called it Belle Vue. The man in charge was Charles Griffin.
Meanwhile, the American government in Washington Territory decided the island was part of one of its counties, and that meant the sheep farm owed property taxes. The Company disagreed, firmly. So in 1855, an American sheriff came over and seized some of the Company's sheep to cover the bill.
So you can see the neighborhood mood. By 1859 there are about eighteen Americans living on the island, plus the Company's farm staff and their animals. Everyone is pretty sure they're standing in their own country. And the animals, very much including the pigs, wander wherever they like.
Imagine you live here. Your cabin. Your fence, or the fence you meant to build. Your potatoes, which you need to get through the winter. And every few days, a Company pig gets in and digs them up.
This is Lyman Cutlar. An American settler, originally from Ohio, who'd come north after the gold rush. Some sources spell his name Cutler.
He'd claimed land right in the middle of the Company's sheep pasture. And that morning, he goes outside and finds the pig in his potatoes. Again. In his own sworn statement, he said the animal had been, quote, at several times a great annoyance.
So he shoots it.
And to his credit, Cutlar then does the neighborly thing. He goes to Charles Griffin, admits it, and offers to pay. He offers ten dollars.
Griffin wants a hundred.
Now, ten dollars in 1859 is real money. It's around a couple of weeks' pay for a working man. One hundred is something like a season's wages. So you're asking, why a hundred? Accounts differ. One version says the Company valued the animal as a prize breeding boar. Other accounts say it was mostly about the larger fight, the one about who had any right to be on this land at all.
And here's where you have to be careful. There's a famous exchange that gets quoted everywhere. Cutlar supposedly says, It was eating my potatoes. And Griffin supposedly snaps back, It's up to you to keep your potatoes out of my pig.
It's a perfect line. It's also, as far as historians can tell, a later retelling. It's not in the documents from that summer. So enjoy it, but file it under the story goes.
What the record does say is that the conversation went badly. And British officials, according to the American settlers, threatened to arrest Cutlar and take him to Victoria for trial.
The pig, for the record, is still dead.
So the American settlers do what you'd do if a foreign power threatened to haul your neighbor off to another country. They call for help. They petition the US Army.
And the petition lands on the desk of exactly the wrong man.
This is Brigadier General William Selby Harney, commander of the Department of Oregon. He's a veteran officer, a hard man and a well-known Anglophobe, meaning he deeply distrusts the British.
He's also the officer who, four years earlier in 1855, had led an attack on a Lakota village at Blue Water Creek in what's now Nebraska. Dozens of Lakota people were killed, including women and children. That's who is now deciding how to handle this.
Harney visits the island, hears the settlers out, and decides this is the moment. He orders a company of the 9th Infantry to go to San Juan Island.
Somebody, somewhere, decides this needs to be bigger.
On July 27th, 1859, a captain lands at a little cove on Griffin Bay with about sixty-four soldiers.
His name is George Edward Pickett. And if that name rings a faint bell, hold onto it. We'll come back to it.
Pickett is a West Point graduate, finished last in his class, and a Mexican War veteran. He's thirty-four, confident, and he puts up a proclamation saying, essentially, this island is American soil, and US courts are the only courts here.
Now put yourself across the water in Victoria.
This is James Douglas, the governor of the British colony of Vancouver Island and a former top man in the Hudson's Bay Company. He has spent decades building British power on this coast. And now an American army captain has planted a flag in what he considers his territory, over a pig.
Douglas is furious. And Douglas has a navy.
Somebody, somewhere, decides this needs to be bigger.
He sends warships. The first serious one is HMS Tribune, a steam frigate with thirty-one guns, under Captain Geoffrey Phipps Hornby. Hornby's orders are to get the Americans off the island, but to avoid an armed clash if he can.
So now stand where Pickett's soldiers stand. You're on a grassy ridge. Below you in the bay, close enough to make out the sailors on deck, a frigate has its guns facing you. You have rifles and a few small field pieces. And your captain is telling the Royal Navy, out loud, that if British troops try to land, he'll fight them. His words were reportedly something to the effect of: we'll make a Bunker Hill of it.
Through August, both sides pile on. The Americans bring in reinforcements, more soldiers and heavier guns, under Lieutenant Colonel Silas Casey. By late August, there are around 460 American soldiers on the island with fourteen cannons.
Out in the bay: five British warships, with about seventy guns and more than two thousand men, including Royal Marines.
Two thousand. On one side of a very small bay. Over a pig. The pig, for the record, is still dead.
And here's the strange part. While the big guns are staring at each other, the ordinary soldiers are kind of bored. They're far from home, it's damp, and the nearest thing to excitement is watching the other side drill. Honestly, the most tense thing on this island for weeks is what's for dinner.
Now, where were we. Right: a 10 dollar pig became a 100 dollar argument, a US general sent soldiers, and the British sent warships. Two thousand men are waiting for someone to give the order.
You'd expect the story to go one way from here. Every condition for a shooting war is in place. Angry governments, armed men, a flag on each hill, and a governor in Victoria who wants the Americans gone.
Except it doesn't.
Here's the part nobody tells you. The Pig War didn't stay peaceful because it was silly. It stayed peaceful because, one after another, individual people looked at their orders and chose not to carry them out the hard way.
First, Captain Hornby. He has the warships. He outguns Pickett many times over. And he holds back. Instead of landing troops, he talks, and he waits for his commanding officer.
That officer is Rear Admiral Robert Lambert Baynes, commander of the Royal Navy's Pacific station. He arrives in early August, takes in the scene, and he is appalled. Governor Douglas wants force. And Baynes tells him no. According to the National Park Service, Baynes said he would not involve two great nations in a war over a squabble about a pig.
Think about how rare that is. A senior commander, with the bigger force, looking at a governor, the man with political authority, and refusing to escalate.
And here's the other twist. Neither government even knew this was happening. News moved by ship. It took weeks for word of the standoff to reach Washington. When it did, President James Buchanan was reportedly stunned. Nobody in Washington had ordered General Harney to start a confrontation with the British Empire.
So Buchanan sends the one man in the US Army with the rank and the reputation to cool things down.
Lieutenant General Winfield Scott. Hero of the Mexican War. Seventy-three years old, enormous, famous for his formality, nicknamed Old Fuss and Feathers. He'd also already helped calm British-American border tensions back in the 1830s.
Scott travels all the way to the Pacific, which takes weeks. He arrives in October 1859, and he opens a polite exchange with Governor Douglas. The deal they work out is almost too simple. Each side cuts down to a single company of about a hundred soldiers. The Americans stay on the south end. The British set up on the north. Both share the island until the diplomats figure out the border.
And Harney? A few months later, he's removed from command.
So imagine you're one of those soldiers. Your war just turned into a posting.
If you're British, you're a Royal Marine at English Camp on Garrison Bay. It's a sheltered little harbor. There's a blockhouse, barracks, a formal garden. If you're American, you're at American Camp on the windy southern prairie, looking out at the strait.
For twelve years, this is the arrangement.
And the strange part is that it mostly works. The two camps visit each other. They celebrate each other's holidays, the Queen's birthday and the Fourth of July. They hold sporting events. The officers dine together. Two armies sent to fight each other end up as neighbors.
But not everyone comes home. Over those years, several men die on the island, from drowning, accidents and illness. No one is killed by the enemy, but it's worth saying out loud that nobody died isn't quite true.
Meanwhile, the country Pickett's soldiers belong to is coming apart.
In 1861, the American Civil War begins. A war caused by slavery, as the seceding states said themselves in their own declarations. Many of the regular Army officers in the West resign to join the Confederacy. And one of them is George Pickett.
Two years later, on July 3rd, 1863, at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, Pickett's division is part of a massive Confederate infantry assault across open ground toward the Union line. It goes down in history as Pickett's Charge. It fails. The casualties are staggering. In about an hour, the Confederates lose more than half of the men who made that charge, killed, wounded or captured.
Think about the distance between those two moments. On San Juan Island, Pickett stood in front of the Royal Navy, and nobody fired. Four years later, in Pennsylvania, almost everybody did.
With the Civil War over and the camps still quietly sharing their island, the two governments finally sign the Treaty of Washington in 1871. Among a long list of disputes, they agree to hand the San Juan question to a neutral referee.
Their choice: the German Emperor, Kaiser Wilhelm I.
Now, think about how strange this is. The border of Washington State gets decided by a German emperor who has almost certainly never seen the Pacific Northwest.
The Kaiser hands the job to a commission of three experts, who meet in Geneva and spend nearly a year going over maps, treaty records and arguments from both sides.
On October 21st, 1872, the decision comes down. The border runs through Haro Strait. The San Juan Islands are American.
And a month later, on November 25th, 1872, the Royal Marines lower their flag at English Camp, pack up and sail home.
That's the end of the Pig War. Thirteen years after the shot in the potato patch.
So what did it cost?
One pig, of course. A few soldiers' lives to accidents and illness over twelve years. A lot of money spent keeping two garrisons on an island where nothing happened. And some careers. Harney's reputation never recovered. Hornby went on to become an admiral of the fleet. Douglas retired as a knight.
And then there are the people this island belonged to before anybody drew a line through the water. The new border cut straight through Coast Salish family and trade networks, which had crossed these straits for generations. Today, the line between the Lummi Nation in Washington and the Saanich and Songhees nations in British Columbia is an international border, because of a treaty none of them signed.
So why does a pig matter?
Because the Pig War is the last time the United States and Britain stood this close to fighting each other. And they didn't. A border fight between two powers, with guns already pointed, got handed to a referee instead.
That's not nothing. Most border disputes in history go the other way.
And because the real lesson isn't about pigs at all. It's about one sentence. The middle of the channel. Someone wrote it in an office thousands of miles away, assumed everyone understood, and didn't check the map. Anyone who's ever signed a lease, a contract, or a group project agreement knows exactly what that's like.
If you go to San Juan Island today, it's a national historical park. And at English Camp, park rangers still raise the British flag. It might be one of the only places on US soil where the US government does that.
So picture it one more time. A foggy morning. A potato patch. A pig that wandered somewhere it shouldn't have. One shot, and then thirteen years of everyone deciding, over and over, not to fire the next one.
The pig, for the record, is still dead. But nobody else had to be.
