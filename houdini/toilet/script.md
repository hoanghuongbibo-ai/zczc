## BEAT 2 — The World Before: Everyone Just… Went
[MUSIC: upbeat, plucky speedrun theme] [VISUAL: Early farming village, people walking away from their homes to relieve themselves outdoors; houses gradually crowd together.]
Let's start with the honest baseline. For most of human history, the toilet was "outside." Pick a spot. Walk away. Done.
And that works great — when you're a small band of people who keep moving. The problem starts when people stop moving. Farming means villages. Villages mean neighbors. Neighbors mean your bathroom spot is now somebody else's front yard.
So the first big rule of toilet history is this: the toilet isn't invented by smart people. It's invented by crowded people.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: RULE #1 — Crowds invent toilets.] [VISUAL: Skara Brae's stone houses, showing stone beds, a central hearth, a stone dresser and small wall cells with drainage channels.]
Which brings us back to Skara Brae. It's a cluster of stone houses on Orkney, occupied roughly from 3100 to 2500 BC — older than Stonehenge and the Great Pyramid. The houses have beds, a hearth and a stone dresser, all made of stone, because Orkney doesn't have a lot of trees.
And some of those little wall cells have drains running beneath them. Archaeologist Gordon Childe, who excavated here in the 1920s, read them as toilets. Today's site interpretation is more careful: some cells were storage, and some "may have been" toilets.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: DEBATED — toilets or storage with drainage?]
So — maybe the oldest indoor bathroom in Britain. Maybe a damp closet. Either way, somebody 5,000 years ago thought hard about water flowing away from where people live.
And that idea? It's the whole video. Because the waste has to go somewhere.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: IT STILL HAS TO GO SOMEWHERE — #1]

## BEAT 3 — The Spark: The First Real Plumbing City
[MAP: South Asia, c. 2500 BC, highlight Mohenjo-daro in what's now Sindh, Pakistan]
Fast forward to around 2500 BC, to the Indus Valley — what's now Pakistan and northwest India. Here sits Mohenjo-daro, pronounced "mo-HEN-jo DAH-ro." It's one of the biggest cities on Earth at the time, and it is obsessed with drainage.
Almost every house has a private bathing area. The house drains feed into covered brick drains along the streets — some big enough for workers to climb in and clear blockages. That's basically a city sewer system, four and a half thousand years ago.
Toilets? Yes — but here's the honest version. Archaeologists have found a couple of brick structures with a hole and a drain that were probably toilets. But according to Indus archaeologist Jonathan Mark Kenoyer, most people probably used old pots set into the ground. Early excavators even mistook some of them for burial urns.
[VISUAL: Archaeologist holding a pot, pausing, slowly putting it down.]
[SKIT] EXCAVATOR (1920s): "A sacred funerary vessel." POT: "Buddy."
So the Indus cities weren't flushing porcelain thrones. What they nailed was the second half of the problem: the pipe. Waste goes out of the house, into a drain, away from people.
And it still has to go somewhere.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: #2]

## BEAT 4a — Rome: Great Pipes, Terrible Sharing
[MAP: Mediterranean, Roman Empire]
Next up: Rome. The Romans love infrastructure. Aqueducts bring water in, and the city's great sewer, the Cloaca Maxima — "the Greatest Sewer" — carries water out toward the Tiber River.
And Romans had public toilets — the Latin word is foricae. Picture a long stone bench with a row of holes. No stalls. No dividers. You're sitting shoulder to shoulder with a stranger, chatting about the chariot races.
[SKIT] ROMAN 1: "So how's the family?" ROMAN 2: "Marcus, I'm begging you, not now."
And then there's the sponge on a stick. The tersorium, or xylospongium — "zy-lo-SPON-gee-um." A sea sponge fixed to a stick, rinsed in water or vinegar.
[ARTIFACT: replica tersorium]
Now, I know what you're thinking: "Please tell me they didn't share it." Here's the honest answer: historians aren't sure what it was for. The traditional view says it was for wiping yourself. More recent research argues it was a toilet brush — for cleaning the latrine, not you. Archaeologist Jennifer Bates put the question perfectly: do you use it to clean yourself, or to clean the toilet?
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: DEBATED]
What we do know: archaeologists have found little ceramic discs called pessoi in Roman-era latrines, likely used for wiping. Which sounds worse. Let's move on.
So Rome had the pipes. What Rome didn't have was any idea what germs were. Shared benches, shared water, no handwashing science. The plumbing was brilliant. The understanding was zero.
And it still has to go somewhere. In Rome's case: straight into the river.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: #3]

## BEAT 4b — The Fall: Back to the Bucket
[MAP: Western Roman Empire fragments, AD 476]
When the Western Roman Empire falls apart in the 400s AD, the big public systems that need a big government to run them fade with it across much of Western Europe.
The result is complicated, but in general: for centuries, a lot of Europeans go back to chamber pots, privies over pits, and cesspits under houses. Castles get the garderobe — basically a small room jutting out of the wall with a hole that drops… outside.
[VISUAL: Castle wall, small overhang, a moat that you suddenly don't want to swim in.]
So the toilet tech didn't advance for a thousand years. It went backwards. Which is wild, and the reason is simple: nobody had a city-sized plan for where it should go.

## BEAT 6a — Sir John Harington: The Queen's Saucy Godson
[VISUAL: Elizabethan court, Queen Elizabeth I on throne.]
Meet Sir John Harington — "HAIR-ing-tun" — courtier, poet, translator, and godson of Queen Elizabeth I. He's also the kind of guy who keeps getting kicked out of court.
According to one account, he got banished for passing around racy translated poetry to the queen's ladies-in-waiting — and his punishment was to translate the entire poem. Which he did.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: REPORTEDLY]
Around the 1590s, at his house in Kelston, near Bath, Harington builds a flushing toilet. He calls it the Ajax — a pun, because "jakes" was slang for a toilet. A cistern above, a bowl, a handle, a valve. Turn it, and water washes everything down into a pit below.
He even installs one for the queen at Richmond Palace. Sources disagree on the year — some say 1592, some say later in the 1590s.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: date debated]
And in 1596, he writes a whole book about it: A New Discourse of a Stale Subject, Called the Metamorphosis of Ajax. Under a pen name that means "hater of filth." It's a toilet manual… that is also a coded attack on people at court. The queen is not amused. He gets banished. Again.
[SKIT] ELIZABETH: "John. You wrote a toilet book. About my friends." HARINGTON: "It's allegorical!" ELIZABETH: "Go allegorize in Somerset."
So — the flush toilet exists in the 1590s. Royal approval. Published instructions. And it… goes nowhere. Why?
Because it had three huge problems. One: one flush reportedly used about 7.5 gallons of water — in a world where water was hauled by hand. Two: there was nothing to block the smell coming back up from the pit. And three — you guessed it — it still had to go somewhere, and "a pit under your house" isn't a plan. [VISUAL: An early flush toilet draining into a pit beneath a house; water poured in by hand, with foul odors rising from the pit.]

## BEAT 6b — Alexander Cumming: The Watchmaker Fixes the Smell
Almost 180 years pass. Then, in 1775, a Scottish watchmaker named Alexander Cumming gets the first patent for a flush toilet. His big idea is tiny: bend the pipe.
[VISUAL: animated S-shaped pipe filling with water.]
That curve — the S-trap — holds a little water in the pipe at all times. That water is a seal. Smell from below can't get past it. Look under your bathroom sink right now and you'll see the great-grandchild of Cumming's bend.
It's the least glamorous invention ever, and it's the one that made indoor toilets bearable.

## BEAT 6c — Okay, Tom. Your Turn.
[CRAPPER bursts in, ecstatic.] Fine. Now, Tom.
Thomas Crapper was a real Victorian plumber in London. Here's the myth: he invented the flush toilet. Nope — that patent belongs to Cumming, nearly a century earlier.
Second myth: the word "crap" comes from his name. Also nope. "Crap" is Middle English and around long before him. The match is a coincidence. The greatest coincidence in the history of plumbing, but a coincidence.
So what did Crapper actually do? He held nine patents, including three for water-closet improvements. He ran a successful company. And he's credited with something genuinely clever: one of the first bathroom-fixture showrooms, around 1870, where people could see toilets displayed out in the open. He made the toilet something you could shop for. [VISUAL: Victorian bathroom showroom around 1870, with Thomas Crapper displaying rows of toilets and plumbing fixtures.]
The famous story that American soldiers in World War I saw "Crapper" on British toilets and brought the word home? That one's popular, but treat it as "reportedly."

## BEAT 7 — The Climax: The City That Flushed Itself Sick
[MUSIC: tempo builds]
Now the toilet finally works. It doesn't stink. Wealthy Londoners start installing them. Progress!
Except — remember the refrain? It still has to go somewhere. And in 1800s London, somewhere means: into old sewers built for rainwater, into cesspits, and from there… into the River Thames. The same river people draw water from. [VISUAL: 19th-century London, open sewage drains flowing into the River Thames as people draw water from its banks.]
More flushing doesn't clean the city. It pours more sewage into the river, faster.
[MUSIC: drops out. Tonal reset.]
[VISUAL: dark, still illustration of a Soho street, 1854. No jokes in this section.]
In late August 1854, cholera breaks out in Soho, in central London. Cholera is an infection spread through contaminated water. It can kill within a day.
A doctor named John Snow believes cholera spreads through water, not through bad air, which is what most experts believed at the time. He maps the deaths house by house. They cluster around one public water pump on Broad Street. In a letter published that September, Snow lists 89 cholera deaths registered in the area in a single week. [VISUAL: Dr. John Snow marking cholera deaths on a street map of Soho, 1854, with red marks clustered around the Broad Street water pump.]
On September 8th, the pump's handle is removed. Later, investigators found a cesspit just a few feet away had been leaking into the well.
Now, the famous version says Snow pulled the handle and saved the city. The honest version is more complicated: Snow himself later learned the outbreak was already declining before the handle came off. His real legacy was the map — and the proof that dirty water kills.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: MYTH vs. RECORD]
And at the time? Many authorities still didn't believe him.
[MUSIC: slowly returns, lighter.]
Here's what did move them. Four years later, in the hot summer of 1858, the Thames smells so bad that newspapers call it "The Great Stink." And the brand-new Houses of Parliament sit right on the river. [VISUAL: The Houses of Parliament beside the polluted River Thames during the Great Stink of 1858, with Londoners covering their noses.]
They try soaking the curtains in chloride of lime. It doesn't work.
[SKIT] MP 1: "Shall we debate the sewage bill?" MP 2 (eyes watering): "Shall we debate it quickly?"
People dying in Soho wasn't enough. Politicians smelling it themselves? That got a sewer funded.
Enter Joseph Bazalgette — "BAZZ-ul-jet" — engineer. His plan: giant "intercepting" sewers running along the Thames, catching London's waste before it reaches the river in the center of town and carrying it far downstream. The main system is finished by 1875. According to Historic England, it involved more than 1,000 miles of new sewers and about 318 million bricks. [VISUAL: Joseph Bazalgette overseeing construction of London's massive brick intercepting sewers, with workers laying bricks beneath the city.]
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: 318,000,000 bricks]
That's enough brick sewer to run from New York to past Chicago. Under one city.
And London only suffers one more cholera outbreak after this, in 1866 — in an area not yet connected to the new system.
That's the moment the toilet becomes safe for everyone. Not when someone invented the flush. When a city finally answered the question: where does it go?

## BEAT 8 — Aftermath & Human Cost
[MUSIC: quiet]
Here's the part that stops being funny.
The toilet you used today isn't a finished story. According to the latest WHO and UNICEF monitoring report, in 2024, 3.4 billion people still lacked safely managed sanitation. That means a toilet whose waste is safely contained or treated — the same problem London had. And 354 million people still had no toilet at all.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: 3.4 billion · WHO/UNICEF JMP, 2025]
Those numbers are improving. Between 2015 and 2024, 1.2 billion people gained safe sanitation. But it's the same lesson Skara Brae, Rome and Soho already taught: a toilet without a safe place for waste to go isn't really a solution.
