## Job one: a wall
[MAP: northeastern Italy in the 500s. Roman towns on the mainland, the lagoon along the coast]
Start with why anyone is out here at all.
Venice tells a very tidy story about itself. At noon on March 25th, in the year 421, the city is founded. Noon, exactly. It's a great story. It also comes from chronicles written more than six hundred years later, about a church that wasn't built until the eleven hundreds.
The real evidence is a letter.
[ARTIFACT: text of Cassiodorus, Variae 12.24, typeset on screen. No manuscript image.]
Around the year 537, a Roman official named Cassiodorus [SAY: kass-ee-oh-DOR-us] writes to the people of the lagoon. He wants them to ship some wine and oil. And along the way he describes how they live. Their houses stand on ground held together with woven branches. Their boats are tied to the walls like animals. Rich and poor eat the same fish. They sell one thing, which is salt. He says they've made their home, quote, "after the manner of water-fowl." [VISUAL: Simple wooden houses on small muddy islands, boats tied outside, villagers fishing and collecting salt]
So there are already people here. Then the mainland gets worse. In 568 an army of Lombards crosses the Alps, the old Roman towns fall one after another, and whole communities move into the marsh for good. [VISUAL: Lombard warriors marching through burning Roman villages as civilians flee toward the marshes]
Why a marsh? Because of what it does to an army.
[MAP: the lagoon, 810. An arrow from the south stalls at the shallows]
In 810, Charlemagne's son Pepin comes to take the lagoon. He captures the outer islands. Then the lagoon stops him. His army can't get across the shallows to the islands in the middle, and he pulls back. The details come from later Venetian writers, who were not exactly neutral. The outcome isn't in doubt. [VISUAL: Pepin's army stranded at the lagoon's edge, facing a vast muddy marsh and shallow water]
The lagoon has its first job. It's a wall. And the lagoon's leaders move their capital behind it, to a cluster of islands at the very center called Rivo Alto. You know it as the Rialto.

## Job two: a foundation
One problem. Rivo Alto is mud.
[VISUAL: cross-section. Water, soft mud, hard clay. Piles driven down one by one]
[SFX: slow wooden hammer blows]
So here's what you do. You take a tree trunk. Oak, larch, alder. You sharpen one end and hammer it straight down through the soft mud. Then another one right beside it. Then another. Thousands of them, packed so tight they brace each other. On top goes a deck of timber, then stone, then a building.
Now, you're probably wondering why a city standing on wood, in water, hasn't rotted away. Rot needs oxygen. Wood sealed in wet mud gets almost none. When the bell tower in St. Mark's Square collapsed in 1902, the piles underneath had been down there about a thousand years. They were still sound. [VISUAL: Old wooden foundation piles buried beneath Venice, preserved in dark waterlogged mud]
Nobody knows the total. Estimates for the Rialto Bridge alone run from twelve to fourteen thousand trunks. And a record from the sixteen hundreds says one church, Santa Maria della Salute [SAY: sah-LOO-tay], took one million, one hundred six thousand, six hundred fifty-seven.
Somebody counted.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Same water. New job.]
Same water. New job. It's a foundation.

## Job three: a highway
[MAP: Venice at the top of the Adriatic. Sea lanes to Constantinople and Alexandria]
So now you have a city with no farmland, no pasture, and no quarry. You do the one thing a lagoon is good for. You get in a boat.
First you sell salt up the rivers. Then you notice where you're sitting. You're at the top of the Adriatic, the farthest north the Mediterranean reaches into Europe. At the other end of that sea lane is Constantinople, capital of the Byzantine Empire, where the pepper and silk of Asia come ashore. Europe wants what the East sells. You can be the one in between. [VISUAL: A Venetian merchant standing between Europe on the left and Constantinople on the right, exchanging goods between the two sides]
In the 1080s you get the deal of a lifetime. The Byzantine emperor is being invaded and needs a navy. Venice has one. In return, he lets Venetian merchants trade across his empire without paying his customs taxes. Historians argue over whether that charter dates to 1082 or 1092. Nobody argues about what it did. [VISUAL: Byzantine emperor meeting Venetian merchants, granting them tax-free trade privileges in exchange for naval support]
[ARTIFACT: Canaletto's painting of the Arsenal gate]
To keep the ships coming, the city builds a walled shipyard called the Arsenal. A hull moves through it in stages. Frame, planking, rigging, oars. Centuries before Detroit. You'll often hear that sixteen thousand people worked there. The historian Robert C. Davis went through the yard's own records for the sixteen hundreds and found closer to three thousand. That's still probably the biggest factory in Europe at the time. In 1574, the story goes, the workers fitted out a war galley for the visiting king of France while he sat through a banquet [VISUAL: Massive stone walls of shipyard, surrounded by water, with ships entering and leaving]
Same water. New job. It's a highway. And Venice owns the trucks.

## The bill
Which is how you end up holding the strangest invoice of the Middle Ages.
[MAP: northern France to Venice, 1201]
In 1201, French knights planning a crusade come to Venice and order a fleet. Enough ships for more than thirty-three thousand men. The price is eighty-five thousand marks of silver. That's about twenty-two tons, roughly double what the king of France took in during a year. Venice builds the fleet. [VISUAL: French crusaders meeting Venetian shipbuilders, with large wooden ships being built in the background]
Then the crusaders arrive, and only about a third of them have come. They're short by more than thirty thousand marks.
The man holding the bill is the doge [SAY: dohj], Venice's ruler, elected for life. His name is Enrico Dandolo [SAY: en-REE-ko DAHN-doh-lo]. He's blind. Nobody recorded his birthday, but he's somewhere between his mid-eighties and his mid-nineties. And he offers a payment plan. Work it off. First, help Venice retake a rebel port across the Adriatic. Then sail to Constantinople and put a young prince on the Byzantine throne. The prince promises to cover everything. [VISUAL: Blind elderly Doge Enrico Dandolo standing before the French crusaders, presenting a payment plan as Venetian ships wait in the harbor]
The prince can't pay either.
[MUSIC: out]
[VISUAL: slow, still frames. Nothing playful on screen.]
In April 1204, the crusaders and the Venetians storm Constantinople. A Christian city. The same city that made Venice rich. For three days it's looted. Churches are stripped. Civilians are killed. The crusade never reaches the Holy Land. [VISUAL: burning city, damaged churches, people flee over the street]
Historians still argue about Dandolo. Did he steer it there from the start, or did a debt just roll downhill, one bad option after another? For the people inside the walls, it made no difference.
Venice comes home with ports, islands, and a large share of a conquered empire. And with four bronze horses, taken from Constantinople's racetrack and set above the door of St. Mark's.
Those are the horses from this morning. Almost. The ones outside today are copies. The originals moved indoors in the 1980s to get out of the polluted air.

## The filter
[MUSIC: returns, lower]
A highway carries whatever is on it.
In 1348 the ships bring plague. Venice loses an estimated third of its people, possibly more, and the disease keeps coming back for generations. [VISUAL: Medieval Venice during the plague, merchant ships at the docks as people carry the sick through empty streets]
So the city gives the water its old job, pointed in a new direction. Seventy-five years after that first outbreak, Venice turns an island in the lagoon into a permanent plague hospital. Then a second island, where arriving ships, crews and cargo have to wait before they're let in. Forty days. In Italian, quaranta giorni [SAY: kwah-RAHN-tah JOR-nee]. That's where the word quarantine comes from. The idea of holding ships offshore started across the Adriatic, in the port we now call Dubrovnik. Venice built the machinery. [VISUAL: Ships anchored at lagoon islands outside Venice, with quarantine hospitals on the islands and the city in the distance]
A wall can also face inward. In 1516 the Senate orders the city's Jewish residents to live on one small island, the site of an old metal foundry, behind gates that are locked every night and watched by guards the residents have to pay for themselves. The Venetian word for foundry was geto [SAY: JEH-toh]. Most scholars think that's where the word ghetto comes from. The gates stayed up for almost three hundred years. [VISUAL: residents gathered inside Venice's walled Ghetto, with locked gates and guards standing outside]

## The letter
By 1500, this looks like a city nothing can touch. Then some mail arrives.
[ARTIFACT: Jacopo de' Barbari's bird's-eye view of Venice, 1500]
A Venetian banker named Girolamo Priuli [SAY: jee-ROH-lah-mo pree-OO-lee] keeps a diary. In 1501 he writes down a report passed along from Egypt. Portuguese ships have reached India. By sea. Around the bottom of Africa. At first he doesn't believe it. When it's confirmed, he writes that Venice's merchants will be, quote, "like a baby without its milk."
[MAP: the old route through Egypt to Venice. Then a new line around Africa to Lisbon]
Think about what just happened. For centuries, Venice's whole advantage was its seat on one particular body of water. Somebody just found a different ocean.
Now, the popular version says Venice collapsed on the spot. It didn't. The old spice routes came back for decades, and economic historians still fight over how hard the Portuguese route hit and how fast. But the direction was set. The world's main street was moving to the Atlantic, and Venice wasn't on it. I'm skipping a lot here. Wars with Genoa, wars with the Ottomans, two more great plagues. They mattered. They didn't change the direction. [VISUAL: A map showing Venice's Mediterranean trade routes fading, while new Portuguese routes expand across the Atlantic, bypassing Venice]

## Job four: a stage
So Venice does what it has always done. It finds the water a new job.
If you can't be where the goods pass through, be where the people come. Carnival stretches on for months. There are masks, music, and visitors from all over Europe. And in 1638 the government opens the Ridotto [SAY: ree-DOT-toh], a gambling house run by the state. It's usually described as Europe's first legal public casino. [VISUAL: Crowded streets of Venice filled with music and Carnival celebrations, with the Ridotto casino opening nearby]
Same water. New job. It's a stage.
A stage is a wonderful thing to own, right up until someone with an army wants it.
[MAP: northern Italy, 1797. French armies closing in]
In 1797 that someone is a twenty-seven-year-old French general named Napoleon Bonaparte. He demands that the Republic dissolve itself. On May 12th, Venice's Great Council votes to do exactly that. The last doge, Ludovico Manin [SAY: loo-doh-VEE-ko mah-NEEN], walks out of the palace two days later. After about eleven hundred years, the Republic is over. [VISUAL: Napoleon Bonaparte standing before Venice's Great Council, demanding the dissolution of the Venetian Republic, the doge leave the palace]
The French take the four horses to Paris. In 1815 the horses come back. The independence doesn't.
Then the wall itself goes. In 1846, under Austrian rule, a railroad bridge opens across the lagoon. Two hundred twenty-two arches, more than two miles long. For the first time in its history, you can reach Venice without a boat. [VISUAL: Long railway bridge stretching across the Venetian lagoon, connecting Venice to the mainland]

## Job five: the threat
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: 1866: Venice becomes part of Italy]
For most of this story, Venice's problem was keeping people out. In the twentieth century, the problem becomes the water.
[VISUAL: mainland factories, pumps, the ground settling by inches]
Factories go up on the mainland shore and pump groundwater out from under the region. The ground compacts. In the twenty years after 1950, the city drops by nearly five inches. And the sea is coming up. Put the two together, and the water at Venice's doorstep stands about a foot higher than it did in 1900.
[ARTIFACT: photographs of the flood of November 4, 1966]
On November 4th, 1966, a storm shoves the Adriatic into the lagoon and holds it there. The tide gauge reads six feet four inches above its zero mark. That's the highest ever recorded. The city stays flooded for most of a day. [VISUAL: storm surge, flooded Venice, water rising above the streets]
Italy's answer takes more than fifty years. Seventy-eight steel gates lie flat on the seabed at the three openings to the lagoon. When a big tide is coming, they fill with air and swing up to shut out the sea. The project runs years late, costs around six billion euros, and comes with a corruption scandal. Then, in October 2020, the gates rise against a real storm tide for the first time. And the city stays dry. [VISUAL: steel floodgates rising from the lagoon, Venice behind the barriers, storm tide outside]
They work. But they were designed to close about five times a year. In their first four and a half years, they closed about a hundred times. One study found that with another twenty inches of sea level rise, they'd be closing roughly once a day.
And here's the part you couldn't see from the square this morning. While everyone watched the water, the people left.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT: 1951: about 175,000 residents. 2025: fewer than 48,000.]
In 1951, about a hundred seventy-five thousand people lived in the historic city. By 2025 the count was under forty-eight thousand. Somewhere between twenty and thirty million visitors arrive every year, depending on who's counting. The old city now has more tourist beds than residents. And since 2024, on the busiest days, a day-tripper pays a fee to walk in. City officials say the resident count misses thousands of people who live here without being registered. Even so, the direction has been the same for seventy years. [VISUAL: Venice population shrinking, tourist crowds growing, hotels outnumbering residents]
Same water. Fifth job. It's the thing people come to look at, and the thing the city has to be protected from.

## Legacy and close
[VISUAL: back to St. Mark's Square at dawn]
So why does this matter if you're never going to live on a lagoon?
Because you might already. Miami. New Orleans. Norfolk. Lower Manhattan. Each of them is starting the conversation Venice has been having for fifteen centuries. What is this water for? What will it cost to keep it where we want it? And who gets to stay?
Venice was never really about canals. It was about people who looked at a swamp nobody wanted and decided the water could work for them. A wall. A foundation. A highway. A stage. Every time the world changed, they changed the job.
A Roman official once looked at them and saw birds nesting on the tide. Fifteen hundred years later the nest is marble, the horses are home, and the tide still comes in twice a day.
The gates can hold the sea for now. The harder question is one this lagoon has never had to answer. What is a city for, when the buildings are saved and almost nobody's home?
